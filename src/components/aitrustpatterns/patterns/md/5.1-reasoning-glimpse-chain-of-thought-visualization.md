# 5.1 Reasoning Glimpse (Chain-of-Thought Visualization)

A structured, human-readable view of how an AI agent is approaching a request, surfaced as a lightweight plan, progress, rationale, and motion cues alongside the final answer.

*Last updated December 2025*

---

## Overview

Reasoning Glimpse is a pattern for making an AI agent’s internal process visible at a human level. Instead of a black-box answer, the agent presents a simple plan, shows progress through that plan (visually and textually), and leaves behind a trace of why it took certain actions.

This pattern typically appears in or near the main conversational surface of an AI-enabled application (e.g., a chat interface, assistant panel, or “Ask AI” sidebar). It gives users a concise, structured summary of what the agent is doing: which steps it plans to take, which tools or data sources it will use, and what has been completed so far.

In chat-centric surfaces, motion plays a critical role. Subtle, continuous animations—such as pulsing status icons, animated step indicators, or skeleton loading states—make the agent feel actively engaged rather than stalled, while textual progress updates provide concrete reassurance.

The core idea is not to expose raw prompts or low-level chain-of-thought, but rather a curated, human-understandable view of the agent’s reasoning, reinforced with visual cues. This helps users:

* Trust that the system is behaving systematically rather than hallucinating.
* Intervene early (e.g., correct misunderstandings, redirect the task) instead of waiting for a wrong answer.
* Audit how a result was produced, especially in regulated or high-impact environments.

**Example Placeholder – Interactive Illustration**

Imagine an embedded interactive component on the pattern page:

* A chat-like panel where a user asks: “Assess whether our new pricing page is compliant with GDPR and CCPA.”

* In the chat stream, the agent message bubble appears with:

  * A “Thinking through this…” line.
  * A continuous, subtle animated indicator (e.g., three pulsing dots plus a small progress bar).

* On the right side, a collapsible “How this is being handled” panel appears, showing:

  1. Parse request and identify relevant regulations.
  2. Collect internal policies and current pricing page copy.
  3. Compare current implementation against regulatory requirements.
  4. Highlight gaps and propose remediation steps.

* As the AI runs:

  * Step badges animate from “Planned” → “In Progress” → “Complete” (e.g., with a progress bar sweep or icon transition).
  * Sub-rows show tool calls (e.g., “Fetched privacy policy.docx,” “Scanned pricing.html”) entering with light motion to signal new activity.

Clicking a step expands a detailed view of what the agent checked, which data was used (at a high level), and any notes or caveats.

## Problem

Without any reasoning visibility, AI-powered systems often feel opaque and unpredictable:

* **Black-box answers**
  Users receive polished outputs with no sense of how they were derived. This undermines trust, especially when the stakes are high (compliance, finance, healthcare, security, strategic decisions).

* **Uncertain progress and waiting anxiety**
  Long-running tasks (multi-step analysis, search across many systems, heavy data processing) appear as a static message or generic spinner. Users do not know whether the system is stuck, making progress, or even understood the request correctly. In chat, a standard “typing” indicator is often too generic and disconnected from actual work.

* **No opportunity to course-correct**
  If the agent misinterprets the request early on, users only discover it at the end, after waiting. There is no structured way to interrupt or steer the process (“Stop looking at marketing data; focus on billing records instead”).

* **Weak auditability and accountability**
  Stakeholders cannot easily answer “How was this recommendation produced?” or “Which documents and tools were used?” This becomes critical in regulated industries and enterprise environments where decisions must be justified.

Reasoning Glimpse addresses these issues by turning an opaque internal process into a visible, controllable, and auditable workflow, with motion and animation reinforcing that the agent is actively working rather than idle.

---

## When to Use This Pattern

* **Complex, multi-step tasks**
  For requests that naturally break into several steps (e.g., research, analysis, planning, sequencing tool calls) and are not instantaneous.

* **High-stakes or regulated domains**
  Compliance, cybersecurity, HR, finance, legal, healthcare, or any area where decisions require traceability and justification.

* **Agentic workflows with tool orchestration**
  When the AI agent calls APIs, executes workflows, modifies data, or coordinates with other services on behalf of the user.

* **Collaborative environments**
  When multiple stakeholders (e.g., analysts, managers, auditors) will review or act on the AI’s output and need shared understanding of how it was produced.

## Probably Overkill When...

* **Low-stakes, single-turn tasks**
  Simple completions like rewriting a sentence, summarizing a short note, or generating quick draft content generally do not require visible step-by-step reasoning or animated progress beyond standard typing indicators.

* **Highly predictable, bounded operations**
  If the UI already makes behavior fully obvious (e.g., structured forms, known data transformations), adding a separate reasoning layer and custom animations may create noise.

* **Derived details are trivial or redundant**
  When steps would simply restate the obvious (“1) Read prompt, 2) Answer prompt”) without adding value, or animations would only repeat existing feedback, the pattern becomes clutter rather than clarity.

## Pattern Anatomy

Reasoning Glimpse typically appears as a combination of:

* A compact, inline representation of the agent’s plan, progress, and activity within the chat or assistant reply, often enhanced with subtle continuous motion to indicate ongoing work.
* An optional expanded view (drawer, side panel, modal, or dedicated tab) that exposes a richer audit trail: steps, tool calls, data sources, and rationale.

### Entry Points

* **Primary entry point: inline in the agent response**
  A small “How this is being handled” or “Steps taken” section beneath the AI reply header, often collapsed by default with a short summary (e.g., “Planned 4 steps · 2 completed so far”) and a minimal animated indicator while work continues.

* **Secondary entry point: side panel or drawer**
  A “View full reasoning” or “View trace” control opens a persistent sidebar showing the full step timeline and detailed activity logs. Subtle motion (e.g., animated progress bar at the top, marching dots under the active step) reinforces which part of the trace is live.

* **Contextual entry point: task or job details**
  For long-running or background tasks, a task detail page or job status modal contains the Reasoning Glimpse timeline so users can revisit how the result was generated. Motion is usually less prominent here, focusing on state changes rather than continuous activity.

### Core Item / Object

The core repeated unit in this pattern is the **Reasoning Step**.

Each Reasoning Step represents one meaningful phase of the agent’s process, rather than every micro-thought. Steps are typically aligned with user-understandable goals, like “Identify applicable regulations” or “Summarize user feedback themes.”

Typical structure:

* **Label**
  A short, action-oriented description such as “Gather relevant documents,” “Analyze trend anomalies,” or “Check policy against GDPR Art. 13.”
  The label communicates goal and scope, not implementation details.

* **Description / statement**
  One or two lines describing what is happening in clear, non-technical language, e.g., “Scanning the latest privacy and security policies in the compliance workspace.”

* **Controls**
  Inline actions such as:

  * Expand / collapse for step-level details.
  * Stop or skip the step (if safe and supported).
  * Retry, re-run with adjusted context, or refine parameters.
  * Flag as incorrect / irrelevant for feedback and continuous improvement.

* **Optional metadata**

  * Status: Planned, In progress, Completed, Skipped, Failed.
  * Time markers: start time, end time, duration.
  * Tool usage: which internal or external tools/services were invoked.
  * Scope: which datasets, documents, or entities were included.
  * Confidence / quality indicators (if interpretable and well-calibrated).
  * Permissions or audit tags (e.g., “Requires admin approval,” “Read-only”).

* **Visual state & motion**

  * Icon state: e.g., hollow circle for Planned, pulsing or rotating ring for In progress, filled checkmark for Completed, warning icon for Failed.
  * Motion pattern:

    * Continuous but subtle activity animation for In progress (e.g., shimmer bar, gently pulsing glow, moving dots).
    * Brief transition animations when states change (e.g., progress bar sweep, icon morph).
    * Static, high-contrast icons for Completed / Failed to reduce visual noise once work is done.

## Behavior & Lifecycle

1. **Initial state: request submitted**

   * The user sends a request that requires non-trivial reasoning or orchestration.
   * The system classifies the request as multi-step and generates an internal plan.
   * In the chat window, an agent message stub appears with:

     * A short status text such as “Planning approach…”.
     * A continuous, low-intensity animation indicating planning activity (e.g., animated skeleton lines, pulsing header).
   * This animation is tightly coupled to actual planning work and replaced quickly by the surfaced plan, avoiding long periods of ambiguous motion.

2. **Plan surfaced as a concise step list**

   * The agent presents a short, numbered list of planned steps (typically 3–7), for example:

     1. Parse requirements, 2) Retrieve relevant documents, 3) Analyze gaps, 4) Draft recommendations.
   * The currently active step is visually distinguished (e.g., highlighted row, animated progress pill, subtle shimmer behind the label).
   * In the chat area, only the high-level list and current step are visible by default. The full timeline is accessible via expansion.

3. **Live progress through steps**

   * As each step starts, the UI updates its status (e.g., “Step 2/4: Retrieving documents (3 of 12 found)”) and switches the step’s visual state to an **active animation**:

     * A small looping progress bar under the step.
     * Moving dots or a subtle pulse on the left step icon.
   * Sub-rows or stacked entries show key activities, such as “Queried contracts database,” “Opened privacy-policy-v3.docx,” or “Ran anomaly detection on last 90 days of data.” Each new activity can enter with a short slide/fade animation to indicate fresh work without overwhelming the user.
   * If a step is long-running, a small progress indicator or count (e.g., “Processing 3 of 8 datasets”) is paired with motion, making the continuous animation feel grounded in observable progress.

4. **User-driven intervention and steering**

   * The interface allows certain safe interventions:

     * Stop the entire task (e.g., “Stop analysis”).
     * Cancel or skip a specific step (e.g., “Skip data enrichment”).
     * Adjust scope mid-run (e.g., “Exclude data before 2023,” “Only consider EU customers”).
   * Controls are visually grouped near the active step to associate them with the ongoing animated state.
   * When the user changes parameters, the reasoning view reflects updated steps (e.g., adjusting the plan or adding a new step such as “Re-run analysis with updated filters”). The UI animates additions/removals in a restrained way (e.g., collapse/expand with a short transition) to preserve orientation.

5. **Completion and consolidation**

   * Once all steps finish (or are intentionally skipped), the agent presents the primary answer or artifact (report, draft, decision support).
   * Underneath, the Reasoning Glimpse shows a completed timeline with each step’s final status and a concise summary of what was found or done in that step.
   * All continuous motion related to the task is removed or reduced to a brief completion animation, leaving a calm, static representation suitable for reading, screenshotting, and sharing.

6. **Persistent audit trail**

   * For tasks that may be revisited, the full trace is stored and accessible from associated entities (e.g., a compliance assessment record, a sales opportunity, a ticket, or a dashboard).
   * The audit trail can be filtered (e.g., “Show only tool calls” or “Show only policy checks”) to support debugging, reviews, and audits.
   * In historical views, motion is generally disabled; only state transitions or expansion/collapse interactions may animate for clarity.

7. **Failure and recovery**

   * If a step fails (e.g., a tool is unavailable, permissions are missing), the reasoning view clearly indicates the failed step, cause (where possible), and impact to the final result.
   * The associated animation changes from continuous “in progress” motion to a stable error state (e.g., icon shake + attention border once, then static warning icon).
   * The agent may adapt the plan (e.g., “Fallback to cached data,” “Skip step 3 and continue with partial analysis”), and the trace records that adjustment with a brief transition animation to show the updated flow.

## States & Variants

### Depth Variants

* **Shallow glimpse (default)**

  * 2–4 short steps displayed inline.
  * A small, continuous progress animation (e.g., pulsing dots or a thin animated bar) signals that the agent is still reasoning.
  * Designed for quick scanning and reassurance without overwhelming the user.
  * Ideal for most everyday B2B/B2C interactions.

* **Expanded reasoning trace**

  * Full list of steps with sub-activities, tool calls, and metadata in a side panel or detailed view.
  * Motion focuses on the active step and new activity entries, not on constant global animation.
  * Used by power users, admins, auditors, or when something looks off and requires investigation.

* **Developer or debug mode**

  * Optional, restricted mode that exposes more technical detail for internal teams (e.g., internal prompts, latency, tool call payload summaries, error codes).
  * May include additional visualizations such as detailed timelines, duration bars, and tool latency graphs.
  * Generally not appropriate for end users but useful for building and monitoring agentic workflows.

### Presentation Variants

* **Inline “How this is being handled” section**
  A compact component under each major AI response, with step chips or a numbered list. A subtle animated element (e.g., a looping “reasoning” bar or pulsing indicator) appears only while the agent is actively working.

* **Timeline or activity log**
  A chronological representation (vertical timeline) aligning steps with timestamps and messages. Motion helps highlight the current position in the timeline and recent additions; older entries remain static.

* **Embedded in task/job cards**
  For asynchronous or background jobs (e.g., nightly analysis, scheduled assessments), Reasoning Glimpse surfaces on the job detail page. Motion is often reduced to incremental progress updates (e.g., percent complete, progress bar) to avoid constant distraction in list views.

## Motion & Visual Feedback Guidelines

* **Continuous but calm activity cues**
  Use small, continuous animations (pulses, shimmers, slow-moving dots) to indicate ongoing reasoning. Avoid fast, bouncing, or highly saturated animations that feel stressful in enterprise workflows.

* **Tie animation to real state**
  Motion should map tightly to actual system states and progress. Animated indicators should start when the agent begins work, update as steps advance, and stop when work completes or fails.

* **Integrate with typing indicators**
  In chat interfaces, Reasoning Glimpse motion should complement (or replace) generic typing indicators. For complex tasks, it is often clearer to show “Reasoning / running tools” animations rather than a typing bubble that implies the agent is only composing text.

* **Respect user preferences and accessibility**
  Honor prefers-reduced-motion settings where available. Provide a motion-light version of the UI where continuous animations are replaced with state changes and static icons.

* **Avoid misleading “infinite” loaders**
  Long-running continuous animation without accompanying textual updates can erode trust. Pair motion with concrete information (e.g., current step, item counts, elapsed time, or stage labels).

* **Limit concurrent animations**
  In multi-agent or multi-task contexts, restrict active motion to the most relevant task(s) and use static indicators for others to prevent visual overload.

---

## Content Guidelines

* **Use human, domain-appropriate language**
  Steps should align with how subject-matter experts describe their work. Avoid exposing internal model jargon (“tokenization,” “vector search embedding”) unless the audience is technical and benefits from that detail.

* **Summarize, do not dump**
  Reasoning Glimpse is not a raw chain-of-thought dump. Each step should capture the intent and result, rather than every intermediate thought:

  * Good: “Compared latest policy against GDPR Articles 13–15; identified missing disclosures about data retention and access rights.”
  * Poor: “Thinking if the policy is compliant… maybe it is… will check again…”

* **Keep steps scoped and actionable**
  Each step should feel like a meaningful unit of work that could, in principle, be performed by a human analyst.

* **Limit step count**
  For most tasks, 3–7 steps strike a balance between granularity and readability. Very long processes should be grouped into phases with nested details available only on demand.

* **Clarify incomplete or partial results**
  If certain steps could not be completed (“2 of 5 documents unavailable”), the wording must clearly indicate gaps and limitations.

* **Surface caveats and assumptions**
  Where possible, steps should note critical assumptions (e.g., “Assuming that the ‘customers’ table only includes active accounts”) so stakeholders can assess validity.

* **Ensure motion and content align**
  Text such as “Finished analyzing documents” should only appear when the corresponding animation has ceased and the step is marked complete. Avoid situations where text and visuals contradict one another.

## Data, Privacy & Security Considerations

* **Redact sensitive content in the reasoning view**
  Steps and sub-activities should avoid leaking personally identifiable information, secrets, or other sensitive data. Instead of showing exact content, indicate type and origin: “Analyzed 12 invoices from the billing system” rather than “Analyzed invoice #12345 for Jane Doe.”

* **Respect permissions and access controls**
  Reasoning Glimpse must not reveal that restricted data exists if the current user is not allowed to know. Use generic language (“Some records were inaccessible”) rather than exposing details that break least-privilege boundaries.

* **Align trace retention with compliance requirements**
  Logging reasoning steps and tool calls creates additional records. These should follow organizational policies for logging, retention, and data minimization, especially in financial, healthcare, or government settings.

* **Distinguish between user input and system inferences**
  For auditability, clearly separate data that was provided by the user from information inferred or retrieved by the agent.

* **Avoid exposing implementation details through animations**
  Animations should not reveal sensitive internal timing or sequencing specifics that could be exploited (e.g., exact durations of security checks) beyond what is appropriate for the domain.

## Interaction Patterns

### Steering & Control

* **Early correction**
  As soon as the planning step appears, the user can correct or refine it: “Remove step 3; comparative benchmark is not needed,” or “Add a step to check internal security guidelines.” Visual feedback (e.g., an animated highlight around the updated step list) helps reinforce that the change took effect.

* **Scoped overrides**
  For individual steps, allow targeted controls such as “Use only last quarter’s data,” “Exclude sandbox environments,” or “Skip external web research.” When an override is applied, animate the affected step briefly (e.g., a short color change or icon morph) to show the new configuration.

* **Pause and resume**
  For long-running operations, a pause/resume capability gives users control in resource-sensitive contexts (e.g., pausing a heavy data scan during peak hours). The animation should change to a paused state (e.g., frozen progress bar) and resume smoothly without jumping.

### Feedback & Learning

* **Step-level feedback**
  Provide simple “Helpful / Not helpful” or “Accurate / Inaccurate” feedback at the step level, feeding into model evaluation and step-planning improvements. Quick micro-animations can acknowledge feedback (e.g., a short thumbs-up highlight) and then return to a calm state.

* **Error acknowledgment and re-run**
  When a step fails, let users retry that step or re-run the entire plan with adjusted constraints, clearly recording the change in the trace. The transition from error to retry should be visually distinct but not jarring (e.g., a brief shake on error, then smooth reset into active animation).

## Example Use Cases

### Compliance Agent

A compliance assistant in a B2B SaaS platform receives a prompt:
“Review this new data-sharing agreement for GDPR, CCPA, and SOC 2 risks.”

Reasoning Glimpse shows:

1. Identify applicable regulatory frameworks for this contract.
2. Extract key clauses related to data processing, retention, access, and sub-processors.
3. Compare clauses against internal policy standards and regulatory requirements.
4. Highlight non-compliant or high-risk areas and draft recommended changes.

As the agent works:

* Step 1 pulses briefly, then transitions to a static “Complete” state.
* Step 2 shows a looping progress bar with “5 of 8 key clauses extracted.”
* The overall header shows a continuous but subtle “Analyzing contract…” animation.

The final output includes a summary plus a complete step timeline for legal and security teams to review.

### Finance / Spend Intelligence Agent

In a spend management application, a finance analyst asks an AI agent:
“Explain why software infrastructure costs spiked in Q2 for the EMEA region.”

Reasoning Glimpse shows:

1. Gather infrastructure spend data for Q1–Q2 in EMEA.
2. Segment spend by vendor, product, and environment.
3. Identify anomalies or large deltas compared to Q1.
4. Correlate anomalies with deployment, usage, or headcount changes.
5. Summarize drivers and suggest follow-up actions.

As steps execute, animated bar segments fill within each step row. The reasoning trace lets stakeholders confirm that the agent considered the correct data sources and did not overlook a critical system or vendor.

### Customer Support Quality Agent

A support leader asks:
“Analyze last month’s tickets and highlight top drivers of customer dissatisfaction.”

Reasoning Glimpse shows:

1. Select tickets closed in the last 30 days with CSAT < 4.
2. Cluster ticket topics and identify most frequent complaint themes.
3. Cross-check themes against product version and release dates.
4. Summarize key patterns and propose mitigations.

In the chat window, a continuous but gentle animation indicates that clustering is still running, while textual updates show progress (“Processing 12k tickets… 78% complete”). Later, when presenting results, the leader can open the reasoning view to show how conclusions were reached, building trust among product and engineering stakeholders.

## Anti-Patterns & Pitfalls

* **Overexposing raw chain-of-thought**
  Dumping unfiltered internal reasoning can confuse users, leak sensitive data, and create security or safety issues. Reasoning Glimpse should always present curated, high-level summaries, regardless of how animated or visually rich the surface is.

* **Pseudoscientific or misleading detail**
  Fabricated metrics or vague language (“Confidence = 97%” without clear meaning) can falsely reinforce trust. All exposed metadata and animations should be grounded, interpretable, and honest about uncertainty. Motion must not be used to “oversell” certainty.

* **Noisy or excessively granular traces**
  Showing every token-level decision or micro-step (“Checking… re-checking… still thinking…”) quickly becomes unusable. Similarly, animating every individual micro-step can make the interface chaotic.

* **Inconsistent behavior across surfaces**
  If some AI features show reasoning traces and others do not, users may misinterpret which capabilities are reliable or auditable. Where appropriate, behavior should be coherent across the product, including the visual language of activity and completion.

* **Ignoring accessibility**
  Complex, animated timelines or narrow side panels may be hard to navigate with screen readers or keyboard-only input. All key information should be accessible with proper semantic structure and ARIA attributes, and motion should respect reduced-motion preferences.

* **Endless motion with no explanation**
  Continuous animation without contextual progress updates can increase anxiety. If the system is stuck or waiting on a dependency, the UI should pause or change the animation and explicitly describe the situation.

## Metrics & Evaluation

To assess whether Reasoning Glimpse is effective:

* **Trust & confidence**

  * Changes in user-reported trust or perceived transparency (via surveys or feedback prompts).
  * Frequency of users expanding reasoning views or revisiting traces in later sessions.
  * Qualitative feedback on whether motion indicators feel informative vs. distracting.

* **Task success & efficiency**

  * Reduction in misaligned outputs or rework due to earlier corrections.
  * Time to task completion compared before/after introducing reasoning visibility and motion cues.
  * Drop in task abandonment rates for longer-running operations.

* **Intervention and steering**

  * Rate of mid-run adjustments (e.g., updated parameters after seeing the plan).
  * Outcomes of tasks where users intervened vs. where they did not.
  * Correlation between animation clarity and frequency of timely interventions.

* **Operational quality**

  * Frequency of step failures and where they occur in the process.
  * Coverage of tool usage and data sources compared to expected workflows.
  * Correlation between perceived “spinner fatigue” and actual task durations.

## Implementation Notes

* **Generate a structured internal plan**
  Internally, represent reasoning steps as a structured plan (e.g., JSON schema) that captures label, description, status, tools, associated data, and visual state. This enables consistent UI rendering, motion control, and analytics.

* **Separate internal reasoning from exposed summaries**
  Maintain a mapping layer that converts internal reasoning artifacts into user-facing descriptions and visual states, ensuring safety and clarity.

* **Design for progressive disclosure**
  Start with a minimal glimpse (including a simple animated indicator) in the main chat area and offer deeper detail in side panels or dedicated views. This keeps primary workflows clean while still supporting power users and auditors.

* **Align with existing design system**
  Use established components (timelines, accordions, badges, progress bars, toasts) to represent steps and statuses so the reasoning view feels like a natural part of the product rather than a separate tool. Extend the design system with a consistent set of reasoning-related motion tokens where needed.

* **Implement motion responsibly**

  * Centralize animation tokens (durations, easing, motion style) to keep behavior consistent across agents and surfaces.
  * Respect system and browser-level accessibility settings for reduced motion.
  * Ensure that all state transitions are understandable even if animation is disabled.

* **Plan for scaling**
  As more agents and tools are introduced, ensure that reasoning traces remain navigable (e.g., filtering by agent, tool, or domain; collapsing older steps; grouping related actions). Motion should scale gracefully—prioritizing the most relevant active work rather than trying to animate everything simultaneously.

By following this pattern, AI-enabled applications can make sophisticated agentic behavior feel understandable, visually active, and trustworthy, while still maintaining safety and protecting sensitive internals of the underlying models.
