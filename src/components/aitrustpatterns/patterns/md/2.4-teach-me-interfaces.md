# 2.4 Teach Me Interfaces

Interfaces that turn user corrections into persistent, inspectable rules so the AI system becomes a jointly configured partner rather than an opaque black box.

*Last updated December 2025*

---

## Overview

“Teach Me” interfaces are lightweight teaching surfaces embedded in AI workflows that turn moments of friction or failure into learning events. Instead of treating corrections as disposable, the system invites the user to articulate “what should happen next time” and converts that into a reusable rule, preference, or training signal.

This pattern leverages a well-known behavioral effect: people tend to value and trust systems they have helped configure or build. When the product clearly shows how user feedback changes future behavior, the AI shifts from being an unpredictable agent to a controllable collaborator.

These interfaces typically appear:

* Immediately after a wrong or suboptimal action (e.g., misrouted ticket, off-target email draft, irrelevant recommendation).
* As inline prompts that ask for small, structured instructions rather than broad free-form training.
* As a “rule center” or preference hub where previous teaching moments can be reviewed, edited, and disabled.

**Example (conceptual, semi-interactive use case)**
In a finance operations dashboard, an AI assistant routes incoming invoices to different queues. When an invoice from a subscription vendor is misrouted, the reviewer corrects it. A small inline panel appears:

> **Should similar invoices be handled differently in the future?**
> **Condition:** When invoice type is `Subscription` and vendor contains `Acme`
> **Action:** Route to `Finance → Subscription Ops` and CC `finance@company.com`
> [Save as rule] [Edit details] [Skip this time]

After saving, a confirmation chip appears:

> ✅ Saved rule: “Subscription invoices from Acme → Subscription Ops + CC finance.” [View all rules]

Later, similar invoices are automatically routed according to the new rule, and the user can see a “learned from corrections” indicator in the activity log.

## Problem

Without “Teach Me” interfaces, AI systems often feel:

* **Unteachable:** Corrections are applied once but not remembered, so the same mistakes repeat and erode trust.
* **Opaque:** Systems adjust behavior in hidden ways (or not at all), leaving users unsure whether feedback had any effect.
* **Costly to configure:** Rich automation is technically possible (e.g., workflow builders, rule engines) but too complex or far removed from the moment of need.
* **Risky:** Users may fear that a single correction will silently impact wide swaths of behavior without any clear scope or way to undo it.

Over time, this leads to resignation (“the AI never learns”), workarounds (manual rework, bypassing the system), and low adoption of agentic features. A pattern is needed that turns everyday corrections into safe, understandable, and reversible learning events.

---

## When to Use This Pattern

* **Repeated workflows with consistent rules:** Domains where similar decisions recur (support triage, invoice handling, CRM routing, task assignment, content tagging).
* **Agentic behavior with real consequences:** Systems that take autonomous actions (send emails, schedule meetings, provision resources, update records) where missteps are costly.
* **Frequent corrections or overrides:** Workflows where logs show recurring manual adjustments that hint at stable underlying rules.
* **Onboarding to automation:** When users are not ready to design full workflows but are comfortable articulating “in this situation, do that instead.”

## Probably Overkill When...

* **One-off or highly bespoke tasks:** Scenarios where cases are nearly always unique and rules rarely apply twice (e.g., one-time crisis response).
* **Low-stakes, low-friction contexts:** Features where an error is trivial to fix and unlikely to recur (e.g., sorting a casual reading list).
* **Stable, explicit configuration already exists:** Products that already require structured rule definition up front (e.g., a dedicated rule builder) and where inline teaching would duplicate that surface.
* **Model behavior is inherently probabilistic and noisy:** Situations where apparent “rules” would be too brittle or encourage overfitting.

## Pattern Anatomy

“Teach Me” interfaces consist of three main parts:

1. **Detection of a teachable moment** where the AI’s behavior is corrected or overridden.
2. **A micro-teaching UI** that captures a small, structured rule or preference.
3. **A rule management surface** where accumulated lessons are visible, editable, and reversible.

### Entry Points

* **Inline after a correction or override**

  * After a misrouted ticket is dragged into a different queue.
  * After a generated response is labeled “Not helpful” or “Off-topic.”
  * After an AI-suggested action is canceled or undone.
* **Proactive prompt in a feedback flow**

  * In rating dialogs: “How should similar cases be handled next time?”
  * In review checklists: “Convert this correction into a rule?”
* **From history, logs, or settings**

  * Activity logs with “Create rule from this correction.”
  * A “Teaching & Rules” page within AI or automation settings.
  * A personalization center aggregating learned preferences.

### Core Item / Object

The core object in this pattern is a **learned rule** or **lesson** that binds conditions to actions or preferences.

* **Label**

  * Human-readable summary of the rule, e.g.:

    * “Subscription invoices → CC Finance”
    * “Enterprise customers → route to Tier 2 Support”
  * Should be concise, domain-specific, and easy to scan in a list.

* **Description / statement**

  * Clear, testable statement describing the logic:

    * “When invoice type is subscription AND amount > $10,000, route to Finance Review and CC [finance@company.com](mailto:finance@company.com).”
  * Often includes an example or snippet of matching data.

* **Controls**

  * **Edit:** Change conditions, actions, or scope.
  * **Enable/disable toggle:** Turn rules on or off without deleting.
  * **Delete / archive:** Remove rules entirely.
  * **Reorder / priority:** Adjust precedence when rules conflict (if applicable).
  * **Scope selector:** Decide whether the rule applies to:

    * Only the current user.
    * A team, group, or entire workspace (with appropriate permissions).

* **Optional metadata**

  * **Created by:** User or system origin.
  * **Created at / last modified at.**
  * **Source event:** “Created from correction on ticket #12345.”
  * **Usage stats:** Number of times applied; last triggered.
  * **Status / confidence:** Active, suggested, deprecated, conflicting, or under review.
  * **Tags / categories:** e.g., “Billing,” “Priority routing,” “Safety.”

### Behavior & Lifecycle

1. **Initial system behavior**

   * The AI performs an action (e.g., routes, drafts, recommends) based on its current model and existing rules.
   * The action is presented with transparent cues (e.g., “AI-assigned queue: Billing Support”).

2. **User correction or override**

   * The user revises the AI’s outcome:

     * Changes a field (e.g., owner, queue, priority).
     * Rewrites or discards a generated message.
     * Reassigns a task or updates a classification.
   * This correction is logged as a candidate “teachable moment.”

3. **Teachable moment detection**

   * The system checks whether:

     * The correction is meaningful (not a minor edit).
     * Similar corrections have occurred before.
     * A stable condition-action pattern is detectable.
   * If so, a micro-teaching UI is prepared with pre-filled context.

4. **Micro-teaching prompt**

   * A lightweight prompt appears, ideally non-blocking:

     * Inline card, toast with an “Improve future behavior” CTA, or side panel.
   * The UI proposes a candidate rule:

     * Conditions derived from the current context (e.g., attributes, customer tier, channel).
     * A default action that mirrors the correction.
   * The user can:

     * Save the suggested rule as-is.
     * Edit the rule details.
     * Dismiss or “Skip for now.”

5. **Rule definition & validation**

   * When editing, the user can:

     * Refine conditions (filters, thresholds, match types).
     * Adjust actions (route, notify, suppress, prioritize).
     * Define scope (personal vs organization-level).
   * The system validates:

     * Conflicts with existing rules.
     * Overly broad conditions (“Apply to all customers”).
     * Potential safety or compliance issues.
   * Warnings or confirmation steps appear when risk is elevated.

6. **Confirmation & transparency**

   * After saving, a clear confirmation is shown:

     * “✅ Saved as a rule: ‘Subscription invoices → always CC finance.’”
   * A link to “View / edit all rules” is visible, reinforcing control.
   * The new rule appears immediately in the rule list with metadata and status.

7. **Execution at runtime**

   * On future events matching the rule:

     * The AI applies the actions defined by the rule.
     * The UI surfaces a small indicator such as:

       * “Applied rule: Subscription invoices → CC Finance.”
     * Controls allow easy override and optional refinement:

       * “Undo & adjust rule,” “Stop using this rule here.”

8. **Feedback & continual refinement**

   * When the rule fires and is overridden:

     * The system may suggest refining or narrowing the rule:

       * “This case did not fit the rule perfectly. Adjust conditions?”
   * Rules can evolve over time through:

     * Updated conditions.
     * Changed actions or scopes.
     * Escalation to more advanced automation surfaces.

9. **Rule management & governance**

   * A centralized “Teaching & Rules” page allows:

     * Browsing, searching, and filtering rules.
     * Bulk enable/disable.
     * Reviewing impact statistics (e.g., tickets rerouted by this rule).
   * Administrators can:

     * Approve or reject organization-wide rules.
     * Enforce certain rules as mandatory.
     * Audit rule creation and changes.

10. **Retirement & cleanup**

    * Rules that are unused or frequently overridden:

      * Are flagged as low-value or obsolete.
      * Can be auto-suggested for archival or deletion.
    * The system can periodically highlight:

      * “3 rules have not been used in 90 days. Review now?”

## Content & Microcopy Guidelines

### Framing the Teaching Prompt

* **Acknowledge friction without blame**

  * “This was updated. Save a rule for similar cases?”
  * Avoid phrasing that implies the user caused an error.
* **Focus on future benefit**

  * “Use this correction to improve future routing.”
  * Highlight how teaching will reduce repetitive work.
* **Keep requests small and concrete**

  * Prefer: “How should similar subscription invoices be handled?”
    Over: “Describe how the system should behave going forward.”

### Rule Summaries

* Use **plain-language, testable statements**:

  * “When ticket topic contains ‘invoice’ AND customer is ‘Enterprise’, route to ‘Billing L2’.”
* Avoid jargon such as “predicate,” “expression,” or “trigger” unless the audience is technical and expects it.
* Provide **examples or sample matches**:

  * “Example that will follow this rule: Invoice #14532 from Acme Cloud.”

### Safety & Scope Language

* Be explicit about **who is affected**:

  * “This rule will apply to tickets that this team receives.”
  * “This preference affects only recommendations shown to this account.”
* Clarify **reversibility**:

  * “Rules can be edited or disabled at any time in Teaching & Rules.”

### Error & Conflict Messaging

* When conflicts arise:

  * “This new rule conflicts with ‘High-value customers → always route to Account Management.’ Choose which rule should apply first.”
* When rules are too broad:

  * “This rule would affect a large number of cases. Consider narrowing the conditions.”

## Implementation Considerations

### Data Model for Rules

* Represent rules as structured objects rather than free-text prompts:

  * Conditions: fields, operators, values, logical groups.
  * Actions: routing, notifications, transformations, constraints.
  * Metadata: scope, creator, timestamps, status.
* Support versioning so changes and rollbacks are traceable.

### Rule Execution & Precedence

* Define a clear evaluation order:

  * Global rules → team rules → personal rules.
  * Or priority-based ordering configurable in the UI.
* Document how ties and conflicts are resolved:

  * First match wins, most specific match wins, or explicit conflict resolution logic.
* Log rule evaluation results:

  * Which rules matched, which were skipped, and why.

### Integration with AI Models

* Combine symbolic rules and statistical models:

  * Use rules to:

    * Constrain actions (hard safety constraints).
    * Nudge model behavior (soft priorities).
    * Post-process outputs (rerouting, filtering, redacting).
* Treat rules as **first-class citizens**:

  * Show when a rule, not the model, drove a decision.
  * Allow rules to override model confidence in defined areas.

### Multi-User and Enterprise Governance

* Support different **scopes and permissions**:

  * Personal rules (owned by a single user).
  * Team rules (editable by managers).
  * Organization-level rules (admin-controlled).
* Provide auditability:

  * “Rule created by Alex Chen on 2025-06-10 from Ticket #483291.”
  * Change history and prior versions.
* Consider approval flows:

  * Proposed rules for shared scopes may require admin review.

### Safety, Privacy, and Compliance

* Avoid storing sensitive content verbatim in rules when possible:

  * Use anonymized patterns or references.
* Ensure rules do not leak information between tenants or teams.
* Allow administrators to define **guardrails**:

  * Certain actions (e.g., sending external communications) may require additional checks before being automated.

## Telemetry & Evaluation

Key indicators of pattern effectiveness include:

* **Adoption metrics**

  * Number and rate of rules created over time.
  * Percentage of corrections that result in rule suggestions.
  * Conversion rate from suggestion → saved rule.

* **Impact on behavior**

  * Reduction in repeated errors for scenarios with rules.
  * Reduction in manual overrides for rule-covered cases.
  * Average time saved per case after rule introduction.

* **Trust and satisfaction signals**

  * User satisfaction ratings for AI-driven features before and after rule adoption.
  * Decrease in tickets or complaints about “the AI never learns.”
  * Qualitative feedback referencing control, predictability, or reliability.

* **Rule health**

  * Percentage of rules frequently triggered vs. rarely used.
  * Frequency of overrides on rule-triggered actions.
  * Number of conflicting or disabled rules.

Design decisions can be iterated by examining where rule suggestions are dismissed, where created rules remain unused, and where overrides cluster around certain conditions or actions.

## Variations & Example Scenarios

### Inline “One-Click” Teaching

* **Scenario:** Support triage board in a B2B SaaS application.
* **Behavior:** When a ticket is dragged from “Product Questions” to “Billing,” a small inline card appears:

  * “Move similar billing tickets here automatically?” [Create rule] [Not now]
* **Best for:** Fast-moving workflows where users do not have time for a detailed rule builder.

### Guided Rule Creation Wizard

* **Scenario:** Finance system handling invoices and purchase orders.
* **Behavior:** After multiple manual corrections, a banner invites the user to configure automations:

  * “These invoices are often reassigned to Subscription Ops. Set up a rule?” → opens a multi-step wizard with previews and impact estimates.
* **Best for:** Higher-stakes processes where more validation and clarity is needed.

### Preference-Based Teaching

* **Scenario:** AI drafting assistant inside a CRM.
* **Behavior:** When a sales representative frequently rewrites greetings or tone, the assistant proposes a style rule:

  * “Use ‘Hi [First Name],’ and keep the tone concise for future outreach.” [Save preference]
* **Best for:** Personalization rules that primarily affect content style and tone.

### Organizational Playbooks as Rules

* **Scenario:** Security incident response platform.
* **Behavior:** After a security analyst handles an incident in a specific way, the system asks:

  * “Convert this response pattern into a playbook for similar incidents?”
* **Best for:** Expert workflows where individual behavior should evolve into shared, standardized practices.

## Anti-Patterns & Smells

* **Overprompting**

  * Asking to “teach” after nearly every minor correction, leading to fatigue and prompt blindness.
* **Hidden impact**

  * Creating broad rules without showing their scope, resulting in surprising system behavior.
* **Opaque failures**

  * Rules that silently fail due to schema changes, permission errors, or conflicts, with no visibility to users.
* **Unstructured “training” only**

  * Accepting free-form feedback (“Please do better next time”) without any structured representation, making it hard to deliver consistent improvements.
* **Irreversible changes**

  * No clear way to find, edit, or disable rules once created.
* **Admin-only learning**

  * Requiring centralized configuration for every rule, blocking frontline experts from encoding their domain knowledge.

## Related Patterns

* **2.1 Guided Onboarding & Tours**

  * Introduces the concept of an AI assistant and its capabilities; “Teach Me” interfaces deepen the relationship by enabling incremental configuration over time.
* **2.2 Explain My Decisions**

  * Explains why the AI acted in a certain way; “Teach Me” adds the ability to change future behavior from that explanation view.
* **2.3 Safe Exploration / Sandbox Mode**

  * Provides a low-risk space to experiment with the AI; “Teach Me” converts successful experiments into persistent behavior.
* **3.x Rule Management & Automation Centers**

  * Offers advanced, centralized control for power users and administrators; “Teach Me” interfaces can act as feeders into this more sophisticated surface.
* **4.x Feedback & Correction Patterns**

  * Captures ratings, comments, and corrections; “Teach Me” patterns define how those inputs become durable, controllable rules rather than transient signals.

This pattern works best when combined with transparent explanations, clear permission and governance models, and a strong sense of reversibility, so users can steadily shape agentic behavior into predictable, trustworthy assistance.
