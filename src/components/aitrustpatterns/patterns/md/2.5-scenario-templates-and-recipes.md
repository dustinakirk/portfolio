# 2.5 Scenario Templates & Recipes

Pre-built, domain-specific AI workflows that help users articulate complex requests by selecting and customizing structured “recipes” instead of starting from a blank prompt.

*Last updated December 2025*

---

## Overview

Scenario Templates & Recipes are pre-configured, multi-step workflows that demonstrate what an agentic AI system can do in concrete business situations. Rather than requiring users to invent prompts from scratch, this pattern presents named scenarios (for example, *Quarterly Business Review*, *Release Readiness Review*, *Incident Postmortem*, *Product Launch Checklist*) that can be instantiated with a small set of inputs.

Each scenario encapsulates:

* A clear goal and expected outputs
* The steps and agents involved
* Required inputs and data sources
* Guardrails, constraints, and assumptions

In a product, this typically appears as a “Templates” or “Recipes” gallery attached to a planning surface, workspace, or AI entry point. Selecting a recipe pre-populates a plan or workflow with editable steps, parameters, and agent assignments, which can then be reviewed, customized, and executed.

> **Example format for an embedded interactive demo:**
> A gallery showing three cards: *Release Readiness Review*, *Customer QBR Prep*, and *Incident Postmortem*. Selecting *Release Readiness Review* opens a side panel with steps (scan Jira, analyze deployment history, compile risks, draft release note), data sources, and required inputs (release name, date, Jira project, target environment). A **“Use this recipe”** button instantiates a plan with editable steps and parameters that can be run by the AI agents.

---

## Problem

Without scenario templates, interaction with an agentic AI system often begins from a blank input field. This creates several issues:

* **Blank-page paralysis and prompt anxiety**
  Users must understand both the domain problem and how to phrase a request for the AI. Many know the outcome they want but struggle to translate it into a structured, multi-step plan.

* **Unclear system capabilities and boundaries**
  Without curated scenarios, it is difficult to infer what the AI can or cannot do, which tools it can access, and how far automation can be safely delegated.

* **Inconsistent outcomes and low repeatability**
  Ad-hoc prompts lead to variable quality, limited reproducibility, and fragile “prompt lore” that lives in chats or personal notes instead of in a shared, governed library.

* **High onboarding and training cost**
  New or non-expert users often depend on power users or documentation to discover effective workflows, increasing support burden and slowing adoption.

Scenario Templates & Recipes address these issues by turning proven, high-value AI workflows into discoverable, guided, and repeatable building blocks.

---

## When to Use This Pattern

* **Multi-step, repeatable workflows**
  When the AI is expected to orchestrate several steps or agents across multiple systems (for example, CRM + issue tracker + analytics) to complete a business outcome.

* **Role- or domain-specific scenarios**
  When different roles (PM, marketer, engineer, CSM) have recurring workflows that benefit from standardization and shared language.

* **High-value but complex tasks**
  When workflows are impactful but non-trivial to describe in a single prompt, such as audits, readiness checks, risk assessments, or executive briefing preparation.

* **Onboarding to an agentic system**
  When the product must quickly demonstrate what is possible with agents, and provide safe, guided “first runs” that build trust.

## Probably Overkill When...

* **Single-step, low-stakes interactions**
  For simple tasks like “rewrite this sentence” or “summarize this article,” inline tools or lightweight prompt suggestions are usually sufficient.

* **Highly exploratory or creative open-ended work**
  In scenarios where structure may be a constraint (for example, blue-sky brainstorming, creative writing), templates should be optional and non-dominant.

* **Obvious, well-known flows already encoded elsewhere**
  If an existing wizard, form, or domain-specific workflow already captures the process clearly, adding a separate AI scenario template may add redundancy and confusion.

## Pattern Anatomy

Scenario Templates & Recipes are composed of a discoverable gallery and detailed, structured “recipe” objects that instantiate plans for agents to execute.

### Entry Points

* **Primary entry point: Templates / Recipes hub**

  * A dedicated “Templates” or “Recipes” tab in the main navigation of the AI workspace or planner.
  * A prominent “Start from template” action in new-plan or new-project flows.

* **Secondary entry point: Empty states and first-run**

  * Empty states for planning canvases, agent orchestration views, or AI dashboards present a small, focused set of “Recommended scenarios.”
  * First-run onboarding surfaces a guided flow that ends in selecting a starter recipe.

* **Contextual entry point: Inline recommendations**

  * In-context prompts such as “Turn this incident into a postmortem” or “Generate a release readiness checklist based on this milestone” that deep-link into a relevant recipe with pre-filled inputs.
  * A small “Browse recipes” affordance near AI text areas or action menus.

### Core Item / Object: Scenario Recipe

The central unit is the **Scenario Recipe**: a reusable, shareable definition of a business workflow that an AI agent or set of agents can execute.

Typical structure:

* **Label / Name**

  * Concise, outcome-oriented and role-aware (for example, “Release Readiness Review”, “Customer QBR Prep”, “Weekly Risk Digest for VP Eng”).

* **Summary / Description**

  * One or two lines explaining the goal, expected outputs, and when it is useful.
  * Should clarify the primary role(s) it targets and what data it touches.

* **Inputs & Parameters**

  * Required inputs: the minimal fields needed to run the scenario (for example, time range, project IDs, account list, environment, severity thresholds).
  * Optional parameters: advanced configuration such as filters, output style, sensitivity thresholds, or notification preferences.

* **Steps & Agents**

  * Ordered, named steps the system will perform, such as:

    * “Gather incidents for the last 90 days from Jira”
    * “Cluster by root cause and impacted services”
    * “Summarize customer-facing impact”
    * “Draft engineering recommendations”
  * For each step: which agent or tool is used, what data is read/written, and what the success condition is.

* **Data Sources & Permissions**

  * Clear indication of systems accessed (for example, Jira, GitHub, CRM, observability platform).
  * Read vs write operations, required scopes, and any approvals or manual checkpoints.

* **Outputs**

  * Primary output format (for example, doc, dashboard, email draft, checklist, tickets).
  * Example snippets or screenshots for high-trust communication of what to expect.

* **Controls**

  * **Use / Run**: Instantiates a plan with fields pre-populated but still editable.
  * **Preview / Expand**: Shows step detail, data access, and outputs before commitment.
  * **Customize**: Allows cloning and editing into a team-specific recipe.
  * **Share / Publish**: Shares within a workspace or organization.
  * **Star / Pin**: Marks as favorite or default for a given role.

* **Optional Metadata**

  * Role tags (PM, SRE, Marketing, CS, Executive).
  * Domain tags (Release Management, Incident Management, Sales, HR).
  * Estimated time to complete or complexity level.
  * Owner, last updated timestamp, and version.
  * Trust markers, such as “Validated by Security,” “Org Standard,” or “Experimental.”

## Behavior & Lifecycle

The pattern follows a lifecycle from discovery to institutionalized reuse.

1. **Discovery & Recommendation**

   * On first exposure to the AI workspace or agents view, the system surfaces a short, curated set of scenarios aligned with the user’s role or current context.
   * In ongoing use, recommendations are driven by recent activity (for example, recent incidents, upcoming releases, expiring contracts) and organizational priorities.

2. **Exploration & Preview**

   * Selecting a recipe opens a detail view (side panel or modal) showing:

     * Goal and output examples
     * Steps and agents involved
     * Required inputs and data sources
     * Any guardrails or manual approvals
   * This step builds trust by exposing the internal plan before execution.

3. **Instantiation & Input Collection**

   * The user triggers **“Use this recipe”**, which:

     * Creates a new plan or workflow populated with all steps and agents.
     * Presents a short form for required inputs; some values may be pre-filled from context (for example, current release, selected account, timeframe).
   * Validation is performed on required fields and permissions (for example, checking access to Jira project or CRM accounts).

4. **Customization & Fit**

   * Before execution, the plan remains fully editable:

     * Steps can be reordered, removed, or extended.
     * Agents can be swapped (for example, from “Draft email” to “Draft doc”).
     * Parameters such as threshold values, data ranges, or communication channels can be adjusted.
   * The system may suggest minor adjustments based on context (for example, adding a step to notify stakeholders).

5. **Execution & Monitoring**

   * Once confirmed, agents begin executing the plan:

     * Progress is tracked step-by-step with clear states such as Pending, Running, Completed, Needs Review, Blocked.
     * Any manual checkpoints (for example, “Approve draft release notes before sending”) are highlighted with call-to-action controls.
   * Logs and justification are accessible for each step to support trust and auditability.

6. **Review & Refinement**

   * After completion, outputs are presented in a review surface:

     * The user can modify generated content, mark steps as successful or needing changes, and optionally re-run specific steps with adjusted parameters.
   * Feedback captured here can be used to gradually improve the underlying recipe (for example, “often edits step 3 for tone → adjust default prompt”).

7. **Save as Custom Recipe**

   * If a particular configuration proves useful, the user can:

     * Save it as a personal recipe.
     * Propose it as a team or org-level standard, subject to admin review.
   * Proven, curated recipes can be upgraded to “standard” with appropriate governance.

8. **Governance, Versioning & Retirement**

   * Admins or recipe owners manage:

     * Version history and change logs (for example, “v1.3: updated to include risk scores from new monitoring tool”).
     * Deprecation and replacement of outdated recipes.
     * Role-based access, including which recipes can write to production systems, create tickets, or send external communications.

## Example Use Cases

### Release Readiness Review (B2B SaaS)

* **Goal:** Assess whether a software release is ready for production.
* **Inputs:** Release name, target date, environments, relevant Jira projects, error-budget thresholds.
* **Agents & Steps:**

  1. Fetch stories and bugs targeted for the release from Jira.
  2. Check test coverage and open critical bugs.
  3. Analyze recent production incidents touching affected services.
  4. Review deployment success rates and rollback history.
  5. Draft a readiness summary, risk list, and recommended go/no-go decision.
  6. Generate executive-level release notes and internal comms draft.

### Incident Postmortem (Ops / SRE)

* **Goal:** Produce a structured incident postmortem document from multiple data sources.
* **Inputs:** Incident ID, time window, impacted services, incident channel link.
* **Agents & Steps:**

  1. Pull incident timeline from alerting and observability tools.
  2. Scrape chat transcript from the incident channel.
  3. Extract key events and decisions into a structured timeline.
  4. Propose root-cause analysis and contributing factors.
  5. Draft follow-up actions and owners, and create tickets where appropriate.
  6. Generate a customer-safe summary for status pages or account managers.

### Customer QBR Prep (CS / Sales)

* **Goal:** Prepare materials for a strategic account review.
* **Inputs:** Account(s), QBR date, primary stakeholders, timeframe (for example, last 90 days).
* **Agents & Steps:**

  1. Pull product usage metrics and feature adoption trends.
  2. Aggregate support tickets and satisfaction scores.
  3. Identify risk indicators and expansion opportunities.
  4. Draft a slide outline or document summarizing value delivered, risks, and recommendations.
  5. Generate a tailored email draft to send materials before the meeting.

### Product Launch Checklist (Marketing / PMM)

* **Goal:** Ensure all tasks for a feature or product launch are planned and tracked.
* **Inputs:** Feature name, launch date, target segment, channels.
* **Agents & Steps:**

  1. Generate a launch checklist across channels (email, in-app, documentation, training).
  2. Map checklist items to owners based on org structure.
  3. Draft core messaging and FAQ from feature spec.
  4. Create tasks in the work management tool for each checklist item.
  5. Set reminders and follow-up tasks leading up to the launch date.

## Variations

* **Inline Micro-Recipes**

  * Small, single-context recipes triggered directly from a document, ticket, or dashboard (for example, “Turn this incident into a customer update email”).

* **Wizard-Style Recipes**

  * Templates that unfold across a multi-step setup wizard, collecting inputs before creating a plan. Suitable when prerequisites and configurations are complex.

* **Visual Workflow Recipes**

  * Recipes rendered as a visual flow (nodes and edges) where each node represents an agent action. Users can drag to add, remove, or reroute steps before execution.

* **Org-Standard vs Personal Recipes**

  * Org-standard recipes governed by administrators with stricter controls.
  * Personal or team recipes where individuals can experiment and iterate without impacting others.

* **Dynamic / Context-Aware Recipes**

  * Templates that adapt steps or defaults based on current context (for example, different steps for P1 vs P3 incidents, or different channels for enterprise vs small accounts).

## Content & Microcopy Guidelines

* **Names**

  * Use concise, action- and outcome-oriented names that reflect real-world tasks (“Prepare VP Eng Risk Digest”) rather than technical jargon (“Incident Data Aggregation v2”).

* **Descriptions**

  * State:

    * What the scenario is for
    * Who it is primarily for (role)
    * What it produces and in what format

* **Inputs**

  * Ask only for the minimum required inputs.
  * Provide clear labels, placeholders, and inline examples (for example, “Jira project key, e.g., ‘PLATFORM’”).

* **Steps**

  * Write steps in plain language with explicit verbs and visible dependencies.
  * Avoid “magic” or opaque steps; explain what is being read or changed.

* **Trust & Safety Messaging**

  * Explicitly call out:

    * Data that will be accessed and whether it is read-only or write-capable.
    * Any external communications that might be sent (for example, emails, Slack messages).
    * Manual approval gates before irreversible actions.

* **Empty States & Recommendations**

  * In empty states, highlight 3–5 high-value recipes, not a long list.
  * Use short taglines to clarify why a scenario is suggested (for example, “Based on recent incidents”, “Popular for PMs”, “Org standard”).

## Interaction & States

### Key UI States

* **Gallery State**

  * Cards or tiles displaying name, short description, primary role, and a “Use” or “Preview” action for each recipe.
  * Filters / facets (role, domain, data source, complexity, verified vs experimental).

* **Detail State**

  * Expanded view with:

    * Goals and expected outputs
    * Steps and agents
    * Data access summary
    * Screenshots or examples (if available)
    * Required inputs and current default values
  * Clear primary action: “Use this recipe” alongside a “Customize” or “Clone” option.

* **Configuration State**

  * Lightweight form collecting required inputs with contextual validation.
  * Optional advanced section for power users.

* **Execution State**

  * Progress indicator per step, with inline explanations.
  * Ability to pause, cancel, or re-run individual steps.
  * Highlighted manual checkpoints requiring human confirmation.

* **Completion State**

  * Summary of what was done, where, and with which outputs.
  * Quick links to generated artifacts (docs, tickets, dashboards).
  * Option to rate or provide feedback on the recipe and save the configuration as a custom variant.

### Interaction Considerations

* Default to **preview and confirmation** before any write operations.
* Reveal details on demand but keep initial exposure simple (especially in onboarding contexts).
* Support **safe undo** or rollback where feasible, and at minimum provide clear guidance on how to revert changes.

## Error Handling & Edge Cases

* **Missing Permissions or Data Access**

  * If access is missing for required systems:

    * Explain which permission is needed and why.
    * Offer to adjust the recipe to a read-only or partial mode, or suggest alternatives.

* **Incomplete Inputs**

  * Highlight missing or invalid fields with actionable error messages, not generic failures.

* **Agent or Tool Failure**

  * If a step fails:

    * Mark the step as Blocked with a clear explanation.
    * Allow retry after adjustment of inputs or parameters.
    * Avoid discarding progress from completed steps.

* **Changes in Underlying Systems**

  * If connected systems change (for example, renamed projects, deleted dashboards):

    * Mark affected recipes as “Needs Attention” and notify owners.
    * Provide a repair flow to update mappings and references.

* **Outdated or Deprecated Recipes**

  * Clearly label deprecated templates and suggest replacements.
  * Prevent running recipes that are known to be unsafe or incompatible with current configurations.

## Data & Telemetry Considerations

To improve recipe quality and trust over time, the product should observe:

* **Discovery & Adoption**

  * Which scenarios are most frequently viewed, instantiated, and completed.
  * Which roles use which templates and in what contexts.

* **Execution Outcomes**

  * Completion rate, failure rate by step, average time to completion.
  * Manual edits applied to generated outputs, especially repeated patterns of edits.

* **User Feedback**

  * Explicit ratings or quick reactions (for example, “Useful”, “Not relevant”, “Needs more detail”).
  * Free-text feedback for recipe owners to refine prompts and steps.

* **Governance Signals**

  * Which templates are commonly cloned and adapted.
  * Where misuse or confusion is detected, prompting content or scope adjustments.

Telemetry should be carefully designed to avoid logging sensitive data while still capturing enough information to improve template design.

## Accessibility

* Ensure that the gallery and detail views are fully navigable via keyboard, with clear focus states.
* Use headings and landmarks so assistive technologies can understand the structure of templates and steps.
* Avoid encoding critical information only in colors; use text labels and patterns for state changes.
* Provide concise but descriptive labels for buttons such as “Use this recipe”, “Preview steps”, and “View data access details”.

---

## Anti-Patterns

* **Recipe as opaque black box**

  * Hiding steps, tools, or data access leads to mistrust. Users should be able to inspect what will happen before committing.

* **Overwhelming, uncurated catalog**

  * A large, undifferentiated list of templates creates confusion. Without role- or context-based curation, it becomes harder to find relevant scenarios.

* **Mandatory templating for simple tasks**

  * Forcing templates even for trivial actions adds friction. Templates should accelerate workflows, not gate basic interactions.

* **Recipes that self-modify without governance**

  * Allowing recipes to silently change based on model behavior or unsupervised feedback risks inconsistency and confusion. Versioning and change logs are essential.

* **Templates that over-promise**

  * Marketing language that implies excessive autonomy (“The AI will manage the entire release”) without clear boundaries increases risk and erodes trust when reality does not match expectations.

## Related Patterns

* **2.1 Guided First-Run Experiences**
  Scenario templates often feature as the backbone of first-run journeys, giving new users a guided path to their first success.

* **2.2 Role-Based Starter Workspaces**
  Role-aware dashboards can surface a curated subset of templates tailored to specific roles.

* **3.x Agent Explainability & Plans**
  Recipes are closely linked to plan visualization and explainability, making agent behavior inspectable before and during execution.

* **3.x Guardrails & Approvals**
  Templates that can write to production systems or external stakeholders should integrate with approval and guardrail patterns.

## Implementation Checklist

**Discovery & IA**

* [ ] A dedicated Templates / Recipes entry point exists in the AI workspace.
* [ ] Empty states and first-run flows surface a focused set of role-relevant scenarios.
* [ ] Filters or facets exist to narrow templates by role, domain, data source, and verification status.

**Recipe Definition**

* [ ] Each recipe has a clear name, concise description, and defined target roles.
* [ ] Inputs and parameters are documented, with clear required vs optional fields.
* [ ] Steps, agents, and tools are explicitly defined, including data access and write operations.
* [ ] Expected outputs and example artifacts are included.

**Trust & Safety**

* [ ] Data sources, permissions, and outbound actions are clearly disclosed.
* [ ] Manual approval gates exist before high-impact actions (for example, sending emails, changing configurations).
* [ ] Recipes with higher risk are flagged and governed (for example, “Org standard – security reviewed”).

**Interaction & UX**

* [ ] Users can preview steps and data access before running a recipe.
* [ ] Plans instantiated from templates are fully editable prior to execution.
* [ ] Progress and errors are visible at step-level with clear actions to retry or adjust.

**Lifecycle & Governance**

* [ ] Recipes support versioning with visible change history.
* [ ] There is a process for promoting personal or team recipes to org-standard variants.
* [ ] Deprecated recipes are clearly marked and safely retired with suggested alternatives.

**Measurement & Iteration**

* [ ] Telemetry is in place to measure adoption, completion, and feedback.
* [ ] A regular review cadence exists for updating and pruning the template library.
* [ ] Signals from edits and failures are used to refine prompts, steps, and defaults.

This pattern, when implemented with careful attention to transparency, governance, and user context, turns abstract agentic capabilities into trusted, repeatable workflows that accelerate adoption and deepen engagement.
