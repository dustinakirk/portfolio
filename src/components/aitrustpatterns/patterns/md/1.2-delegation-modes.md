# 1.2 Delegation Modes (Advisor → Co‑Pilot → Autopilot)

Expose clear, named levels of AI autonomy so users can decide how much control to delegate—from suggestions only, to draft-and-confirm, to fully automated execution within guardrails.

*Last updated December 2025*

---

## Overview

Delegation Modes make the **level of autonomy** of an AI agent explicit. Instead of a vague “smart automation” toggle, the product provides clear options for how the agent behaves:

* **Advisor** – read-only, suggestions only
* **Co‑Pilot** – drafts and proposes actions, human approval required
* **Autopilot** – executes within defined guardrails, with monitoring and logs

This pattern appears wherever an agent can change data, communicate with customers, move money, or trigger workflows—email assistants, support triage, CRM workflow agents, finance automations, security alert handlers, and similar agentic systems in B2B and B2C web products.

By aligning with established “levels of autonomy” and human‑in‑the‑loop concepts, Delegation Modes provide a shared language for “who is in charge” at any moment. This reduces anxiety, enables gradual trust-building, and makes it easier for organizations to adopt higher degrees of automation without losing oversight.

---

## Problem

Without explicit delegation modes, it is often unclear **how much power** the agent actually has.

* **Autonomy is ambiguous and unsettling.**
  Many AI features are introduced as “assistants” or “co‑pilots”, but it is not obvious whether they can send messages, update records, or move money on their own. Hidden or poorly-labeled automation leads to fear, disengagement, or outright rejection of AI features.

* **All‑or‑nothing workflows cause friction.**
  Systems tend to default either to a constant approval requirement (approval for every micro‑step) or to fully automatic behaviors that occasionally do something surprising or incorrect. There is no way to tune the tradeoff between speed and control.

* **Organizations cannot scale trust systematically.**
  Different domains and roles require different levels of control. Support replies, refunds, access management, and HR workflows all carry different risks. Without a structured autonomy model, it is difficult to set policies (for example, “Support replies can be autonomous, refunds cannot”), communicate expectations, or audit what happened.

Delegation Modes introduce a **simple, legible control surface** that resolves this ambiguity and lets trust scale over time.

---

## When to Use This Pattern

* **Agents can take real, potentially irreversible actions.**
  For example: sending emails or messages, updating records, deploying code, changing configurations, creating tasks, or moving money.

* **The same workflow has both low‑ and high‑risk variants.**
  For example: standard support replies vs. VIP accounts, small vs. large financial transactions, internal vs. customer‑visible changes.

* **Trust is expected to evolve over time.**
  New installations and teams often want Advisor mode at first, then gradually shift stable parts of the workflow to Co‑Pilot, and eventually move safe, well‑understood segments into Autopilot.

* **Human‑in‑the‑loop behavior is required for compliance or safety.**
  Regulated domains (finance, healthcare, HR, security, legal, critical operations) often require clear human approval points and auditable logs of AI decisions.

---

## Probably Overkill When...

* **The agent is purely read‑only or exploratory.**
  For example: document Q&A, analytics summaries, search assistants, or similar features that never perform write actions.

* **The UI already forces explicit confirmation on every action, and actions are low‑impact.**
  For example: “generate title”, “suggest alt text”, or one‑off content suggestions in low‑risk surfaces.

* **The derived outputs are trivial and fully reversible.**
  In personal note‑taking, quick drafting, or private ideation spaces, a simple “Auto‑apply suggestions” checkbox may be sufficient.

## Pattern Anatomy

At its core, this pattern is a **mode selector** that binds a clear label and behavioral contract to an agent or workflow, plus persistent indicators and logs that reflect that choice. It is typically positioned near the main “Run agent” surfaces and in the agent’s configuration UI.

### Entry Points

* **Primary entry point – Agent or workflow settings**
  A “Delegation mode” control appears in the agent’s main configuration, workflow builder, or integration settings (for example, “Support Inbox Agent → Delegation mode: Advisor / Co‑Pilot / Autopilot”).

* **Secondary entry point – Inline near execution controls**
  A segmented control or dropdown sits next to the primary action:

  * `Mode: Advisor ▾` near a “Run agent” button
  * A chip in the header such as `Co‑Pilot • Needs approval`

* **Contextual entry point – From banners, toasts, or logs**

  * A banner after repeated approvals: “Many similar actions were approved. Consider switching this workflow to Autopilot.”
  * An alert in case of issues: “Autopilot paused after several reverted actions. Review and adjust settings.”

### Core Item / Object

The main unit is a **Delegation Mode configuration** for a given scope (agent, workflow, dataset, inbox, project, or environment).

* **Label**
  Standard labels, with short descriptors:

  * *Advisor* – “Suggestions only”
  * *Co‑Pilot* – “Drafts; human approval required”
  * *Autopilot* – “Executes within guardrails”

* **Description / statement**
  A concise, domain‑specific sentence describing behavior:

  * Advisor: “Reads data and suggests actions; no changes are made automatically.”
  * Co‑Pilot: “Drafts updates and messages and places them in a review queue.”
  * Autopilot: “Executes actions automatically within specified limits.”

* **Controls**

  * Radio group, segmented control, or dropdown for the three modes
  * Optional per‑task override (for example, “Run this task in Advisor only”)
  * “View guardrails” or “See what Autopilot can and cannot do”
  * “Pause Autopilot” or “Downgrade to Co‑Pilot” buttons within logs or status surfaces

* **Optional metadata**

  * **Scope:** the resource or surface affected (for example, “Support inbox: Tier‑3 tickets only”)
  * **Risk level:** “Recommended for low‑risk / internal‑only operations”
  * **Owner:** which user or role configured the mode
  * **Status:** Active, Paused, Pending approval, Under review
  * **Last change:** timestamp plus the actor who changed it (for auditability)

## Behavior & Lifecycle

Delegation Modes can be understood as a **lifecycle of trust** between humans and agents.

1. **Initial state (Advisor by default)**

   * When the agent is first enabled, the default mode is usually **Advisor** or the lowest‑risk alternative.
   * A short explainer clarifies that the agent will make suggestions only and that no automatic changes will be made at this stage.
   * This default reduces perceived risk and encourages initial experimentation.

2. **Exploration and upgrade to Co‑Pilot**

   * As teams repeatedly approve similar actions (for example, the same type of reply or update), the system can suggest moving that portion of the workflow to **Co‑Pilot**.
   * Switching to Co‑Pilot remains reversible, and the UI emphasizes that humans still approve every change before it goes live.
   * Co‑Pilot often introduces batching: multiple draft actions are collected and reviewed together.

3. **Task‑level overrides**

   * At run time, a task‑level override allows a specific job to run at a different mode than the default:

     * “Run once in Advisor mode”
     * “This campaign: Co‑Pilot (review required)”
     * “For low‑risk tickets only, use Autopilot”
   * The **effective mode** for the current run is clearly indicated, especially when it differs from the agent or workspace default.

4. **Autopilot activation with explicit confirmation**

   * Enabling **Autopilot** requires an explicit confirmation step summarizing:

     * The scope of impact (which records, inboxes, projects, or environments are affected)
     * Guardrails and limits (for example, “No destructive operations”, “Maximum refund: $100”, “Exclude VIP customers”)
     * Monitoring and rollback options
   * This step often appears as a compact wizard with a summary screen: “Here is what Autopilot is permitted to do in this context.”

5. **Run‑time behavior per mode**

   * **Advisor**

     * Provides inline suggestions, draft content, and recommendations.
     * Does not perform direct writes or actions.
     * Failure is low‑risk: suggestions may be omitted or marked as low confidence.

   * **Co‑Pilot**

     * Drafts actions (emails, CRM updates, routing decisions, configuration changes) and places them into a review queue.
     * Supports batch review: “12 proposed replies ready for review.”
     * Highlights risky or impactful actions with additional emphasis before approval.

   * **Autopilot**

     * Executes actions automatically according to configured guardrails, often on schedules or in response to events.
     * Logs every action with inputs, reasoning artifacts (if available), and outcomes.
     * Routes edge cases and low‑confidence decisions “up” to Co‑Pilot behavior for review rather than executing blindly.

6. **Exceptions, escalation, and pausing**

   * Thresholds are defined where Autopilot must escalate or defer:

     * Unusual patterns or anomalies
     * Violations of policy or guardrail constraints
     * Low confidence or uncertain situations
     * High‑value entities (VIP customers, large payments, security‑sensitive assets)
   * In the presence of repeated issues (for example, multiple reversed actions), the system can automatically pause Autopilot for that scope and notify the responsible owner.
   * Downgrading from Autopilot to Co‑Pilot or Advisor is always available via prominent controls.

7. **Evolution over time**

   * Analytics report on approval rates, error rates, time saved, and common escalation reasons for each mode.
   * The system can recommend mode changes based on observed behavior:

     * High approval rates and low risk → suggest Autopilot for that subset of tasks
     * Frequent reversions or escalations → suggest downgrading to Co‑Pilot or Advisor
   * Administrators can define global policies, such as:

     * “Autopilot disabled for payments above a threshold.”
     * “Only administrators can enable Autopilot on customer‑visible channels.”
     * “Default to Advisor for workflows in regulatory domains.”

## Content & Microcopy Guidelines

* **Use intuitive metaphors and stable naming.**
  Terms such as “Advisor”, “Co‑Pilot”, and “Autopilot” leverage familiar metaphors from aviation and existing AI products. When different labels are required, maintain a similarly clear progression (for example, “Review‑only / Assist / Auto‑run”).

* **Make the behavioral contract explicit.**
  Each mode description should answer:

  * What the agent is allowed to do
  * What the agent will never do in that mode
  * Whether human approval is required before actions take effect

  For example:

  * Advisor: “All outputs are suggestions; nothing is applied automatically.”
  * Co‑Pilot: “Actions are drafted and must be approved before they go live.”
  * Autopilot: “Actions are executed automatically within defined limits.”

* **Write from an end‑user perspective, not an internal one.**
  Microcopy should describe the collaboration model in human terms (who reviews, when changes occur, and under what constraints) rather than implementation details.

* **Show scope in plain language.**
  For example: “Autopilot applies only to tickets labeled ‘FAQ’ and ‘Tier‑3’ and does not affect billing data.”

* **Avoid dark patterns or ambiguous defaults.**
  Do not quietly auto‑upgrade existing configurations to Autopilot as part of a product update. Any change that increases autonomy should be clearly explained and explicitly confirmed.

## Implementation Notes (Product & Engineering)

* **Data model and scope**

  * Represent Delegation Mode as part of the configuration object for:

    * Agents (per agent)
    * Workflows or playbooks (per workflow)
    * Resources or segments (per inbox, project, customer segment, etc.)
  * Persist:

    * `mode` (Advisor, Co‑Pilot, Autopilot)
    * `scope` (resource identifiers or filters)
    * `guardrails` (constraints and thresholds)
    * `owner` (who configured it)
    * `source` (policy vs. individual preference)

* **Permissions and roles**

  * Enforce that some modes require certain roles or permissions (for example, Autopilot for financial actions restricted to finance or admin roles).
  * Allow organizations to disable Autopilot for certain domains entirely for policy or compliance reasons.
  * Support per‑user overrides that tighten autonomy (for example, an individual can opt to keep their own workflows in Advisor even when a team default is Co‑Pilot).

* **Guardrails mapping**

  * For each mode, explicitly map what the agent can do:

    * Allowed tools and APIs
    * Maximum actions per time interval
    * Monetary limits or quantity limits
    * Excluded entities (such as VIP lists, specific projects, or restricted records)
  * Associate confidence thresholds and additional checks with higher‑risk operations.

* **Observability and audit**

  * Log every AI‑initiated action with:

    * Mode in effect at execution time
    * Inputs, prompts, or triggering events
    * Outputs or actions taken
    * Any human approvals, edits, or reversions
  * Provide UI for:

    * Filtering actions by mode (Advisor / Co‑Pilot / Autopilot)
    * Inspecting individual actions and their context
    * Undoing or mitigating actions where reversal is possible

* **Resilience and fail‑safe design**

  * On model errors, external service failures, or inconsistent signals, degrade toward safer modes (Co‑Pilot or Advisor) instead of continuing Autopilot execution.
  * Prefer fail‑closed behavior for irreversible or high‑risk actions, and fail‑open visibility for issues (for example, surfacing alerts rather than silently skipping).

## Example Scenarios

### 1) Support Inbox Triage (B2B SaaS)

* **Advisor**
  The agent reads incoming tickets and suggests tags, priorities, and draft replies. Human agents apply, modify, or ignore these suggestions as needed.

* **Co‑Pilot**
  The agent auto‑fills tags and drafts replies into a review queue. Support staff review and approve or edit the drafts, often in batch.

* **Autopilot**
  For low‑risk ticket types (such as password resets, shipping‑status questions), the agent automatically sends replies and resolves tickets according to predefined templates and limits. Complex or sensitive messages are escalated for human review.

### 2) Sales / CRM Pipeline Assistant

* **Advisor**
  The agent suggests next‑best actions, contact prioritization, and proposed stage changes but does not alter records.

* **Co‑Pilot**
  The agent drafts follow‑up emails, proposes stage changes, and groups these into a queue for sales representatives or operations staff to approve and apply.

* **Autopilot**
  The agent automatically updates opportunity stages based on engagement signals (meetings, email replies) and sends low‑stakes follow‑ups (for example, gentle reminders) according to predefined criteria, while leaving high‑value or strategic moves for human decision.

### 3) Portfolio or Trading Assistant (B2C or Prosumer)

* **Advisor**
  The agent analyzes a portfolio and proposes trades and rebalancing actions, presenting them as recommendations only.

* **Co‑Pilot**
  The agent constructs an order basket aligned with a chosen strategy and risk profile; the human investor approves or edits before submission.

* **Autopilot**
  The agent executes trades automatically within defined parameters (allocation bands, maximum order sizes, asset restrictions), escalates unusual market conditions, and produces logs and reports for transparency.

### 4) Recruiting Assistant

* **Advisor**
  The agent ranks candidates, highlights promising profiles, and suggests communication templates for outreach, while recruiters execute all actions manually.

* **Co‑Pilot**
  The agent prepares shortlists and drafts outreach sequences using pre‑approved templates. Recruiters review and launch these campaigns.

* **Autopilot**
  For pre‑approved candidate segments and template sets, the agent sends outreach automatically, monitors responses, and flags interested candidates for recruiter follow‑up, while keeping sensitive communications or senior roles in Co‑Pilot or Advisor modes.

## Risks & Anti‑Patterns

* **Hidden Autopilot behavior**
  Autopilot is effectively active, but the UI does not clearly communicate this. When the system acts on its own in unexpected ways, trust is quickly eroded.

* **Vague or purely marketing‑oriented naming**
  Labels such as “Smart mode” or “Turbo AI” do not communicate actual capabilities or limits. Clear operational contracts are more important than catchy names.

* **Excessive configuration complexity**
  Exposing dozens of fine‑grained toggles (per action type, entity, or segment) without a coherent mode model leads to configuration fatigue. Delegation Modes should be the primary abstraction, with advanced controls grouped under each mode as needed.

* **Irreversible actions without appropriate guardrails**
  Allowing Autopilot to perform destructive or high‑impact actions (such as deleting data, sending bulk campaigns, or transferring funds) without limits, approvals, or rollback mechanisms is a major risk.

* **Silent auto‑upgrades to higher autonomy**
  Automatically migrating existing configurations from Advisor to Co‑Pilot or Autopilot as part of a release, without explicit consent, undermines long‑term trust even if short‑term metrics look positive.
