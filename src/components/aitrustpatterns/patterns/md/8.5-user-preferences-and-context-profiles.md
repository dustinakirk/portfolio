# 8.5 User Preference & Context Profiles

A persistent, user-controlled “about me” profile that captures goals, background, working style, and constraints, applied across agents and sessions so every interaction feels tailored, consistent, and respectful of the user’s intent and boundaries.

*Last updated December 2025*

---

## Overview

User Preference & Context Profiles define **who the user is**, **what they’re trying to achieve**, and **how they like to work**—separate from how any specific AI agent behaves.

Where 8.2 “Preference & Persona Settings” focuses on **agent personas** (“How should this AI act?”), this pattern focuses on the **user-side profile** (“Who am I, what do I care about, and how should AI adapt to me?”).

In a multi‑agent, chat-based ecosystem, each interaction becomes:

> **Agent Persona (8.2)**
>
> * **User Preference & Context Profile (8.5)**
> * **Task / Conversation Instructions**
> * **Memory / History (8.1)**

When implemented well, User Preference & Context Profiles:

* Prevent repetitive “Let me tell you about myself” prompts.
* Make personalization feel **explicit and controllable**, not mysterious.
* Allow a **team of agents** to share a common understanding of the user.
* Improve trust by making it clear what is remembered, how, and where it’s applied.

**Example concept:**
A product suite offers a “My AI Profile” panel accessible from any chat. The user sets:

* Role: “Principal Product Designer”
* Domains: “Enterprise SaaS, B2B analytics”
* Goals: “Ship high‑quality design specs faster; reduce rework.”
* Working style: “Start with high‑level options, then go deep,” “Prefer diagrams and tables,” “Call out risks explicitly.”
* Accessibility: “Use clear headings; avoid dense walls of text.”

Every agent—design assistant, analytics explainer, meeting summarizer—automatically sees and respects this profile unless the user temporarily disables it for a particular thread.

---

## Problem

Without an explicit, user-controlled preference & context profile:

* **Users repeat the same biography and preferences**
  “I’m a staff engineer… I know X, but not Y… I prefer concise answers…” across every chat, agent, or session. This is tedious and error-prone.

* **Personalization feels opaque or creepy**
  Systems infer preferences from behavior and apply them silently. When the AI “just knows things” about the user, but there’s no clear surface to inspect or edit, trust erodes.

* **Multi-agent experiences become fragmented**
  Different agents (e.g., a code assistant vs. a meeting assistant) each ask for the same basics, or behave as if the user is a beginner in one place and an expert in another.

* **User goals are lost between sessions**
  The AI optimizes for one-off answers instead of a user’s ongoing goals (“help me become better at X over the next quarter,” “help me manage my time constraints”).

This pattern addresses these issues by creating a **first‑class, inspectable profile** that agents consult and that users can control, override, or delete.

---

## When to Use This Pattern

Use User Preference & Context Profiles when:

* The product includes **one or more AI agents** that users return to regularly (daily/weekly).
* The system supports **multiple roles or skill levels** (novices and experts, ICs and leaders).
* The product crosses **multiple surfaces or tools** (web app, IDE, inbox, analytics, etc.).
* Users have **stable, long‑lived goals** (e.g., “grow my sales pipeline,” “learn product analytics,” “refactor legacy codebase”).
* You want to give users a clear way to understand and shape how “personalization” works.

### Probably Overkill When...

* The AI is **single‑use or anonymous**, with no login or persistent state.
* Tasks are **highly constrained and role-agnostic** (e.g., “paste log, get error explanation”).
* The product already asks for necessary context **inline per task** and does not benefit from long‑term memory.
* Organizational or regulatory requirements **forbid** storing user-level preferences beyond a session.

## Pattern Anatomy

User Preference & Context Profiles revolve around a durable, user-owned configuration object that can be referenced by any agent in the system.

### Entry Points

* **Global “My AI Profile” entry point**

  * In account or profile settings under “AI & Personalization,” “My AI Profile,” or “About Me.”
  * Often surfaced alongside general preferences (language, notifications).

* **In-chat access**

  * An “About you” chip or icon in the chat header: “Using your AI profile.” Clicking opens a side panel to inspect or edit.
  * Inline banner on first use: “This agent will use your AI Profile (role, goals, preferences). [Review] [Turn off].”

* **Contextual triggers**

  * After repeated self-description: “You’ve described your role and preferences a few times. Save this as part of your AI Profile?”
  * When adding a new agent: “Share your existing AI Profile with this new assistant? [Yes, with options] [No, keep separate].”

---

### Core Item / Object

The main unit is the **User Preference & Context Profile** (often just “AI Profile” in the UI).

**Core elements:**

* **Identity & role**

  * Title, level, domain (“Principal Designer in B2B SaaS,” “Finance Manager,” “Student”).
  * Optional: multiple role contexts (“As a manager,” “As an IC”).

* **Goals**

  * Near‑term goals (e.g., “ship redesign by Q2”) and ongoing goals (“improve SQL skills,” “reduce time spent on reports”).
  * Guidance on priority (“Optimize for speed vs. depth,” “Prioritize learning over shortcuts”).

* **Knowledge & skill assumptions**

  * Self‑rated proficiency: “Product analytics: advanced,” “Python: intermediate,” “Cloud security: novice.”
  * Preferences like “Explain new concepts with analogies,” or “Skip basics unless I ask.”

* **Working style & format preferences**

  * Preferred detail level, structure, and pacing.
  * Preferred formats (tables, diagrams, code samples, bullet points).
  * “Always show assumptions,” “Offer 2–3 options before recommendations.”

* **Constraints & boundaries**

  * Time constraints (“Assume I have limited time; suggest quickest path.”).
  * Risk / speculation preferences (within safety limits).
  * Budget thresholds, geographic constraints, or regulatory constraints relevant to the user.

* **Tools, environment & ecosystem**

  * Common products & stacks (“We use Jira, Figma, Notion,” “Primary language: TypeScript”).
  * Integration hints (“Prefer updating existing tickets vs. creating new ones,” “Use markdown for documentation drafts”).

* **Accessibility & communication preferences**

  * Reading preferences (plain language, short paragraphs, step-by-step).
  * Neurodiversity-friendly options (reduced ambiguity, explicit summaries).
  * Localization (language, spelling, date/time formats).

**Controls:**

* Edit / configure (opens full profile).
* Quick toggles per agent or conversation:

  * “Apply my profile to this agent.”
  * “Pause my profile for this conversation.”
* Optional: **multiple profiles** (e.g., “Work profile,” “Side project profile,” “Personal learning profile”) with clear active state.

**Metadata:**

* Scope: Always personal, but may have **workspace-aware fields** (“goals in this workspace”).
* Sharing: visible to user only by default; optionally share certain fields with teammates/manager, with explicit consent.
* Last updated, last used.
* Indicators for sensitive fields (e.g., health/disability disclosure) with special handling.

## Behavior & Lifecycle

1. **Default state**

   * New users start with **no profile or a minimal skeleton** (e.g., name, language).
   * System behaves sensibly without profile, but prompts to create one after it detects repeated patterns.

2. **Initial setup / creation**

   * Introduced at a **relevant moment** (e.g., after 3+ chats or first time using multiple agents).
   * Light, skippable questions:

     * “What’s your role?”
     * “What are you mainly trying to accomplish with this assistant?”
     * “How familiar are you with this domain?”
   * Provide presets with quick choices: “I’m a beginner / intermediate / advanced,” “Optimize for speed / learning / thoroughness.”

3. **Profile application during interactions**

   * When active, the profile is **automatically merged** into each agent’s prompt or configuration.
   * The chat UI shows a small indicator: “Using your AI Profile: [Role], [Goal], [Style].”
   * Agents may selectively use only relevant fields (e.g., meeting summaries use working style & goals, not tooling preferences).

4. **Overrides & ad‑hoc instructions**

   * Users can override profile for a single task:

     * “Ignore my usual settings; treat me as a beginner on this topic.”
     * A checkbox or quick action: “Temporarily disable AI Profile for this thread.”
   * Conversation-level instructions coexist with the profile; precedence should be predictable (e.g., “task instructions > profile defaults”).

5. **Cross-agent & cross-surface propagation**

   * By default, all agents in the same workspace share the **same user profile**, unless the user opts out for a particular agent.
   * Differences per agent are clearly explained:

     * “This agent uses your profile plus additional domain assumptions.”

6. **Review, refinement & nudges**

   * Periodic and context-aware prompts:

     * “It seems you’re comfortable with advanced topics now. Update your expertise level?”
     * “You often ask for more detail—change your default detail level?”
   * Edits are reflected immediately and visible across agents.

7. **Archival & deletion**

   * Users can:

     * Clear specific sections (e.g., goals, tools) without deleting the entire profile.
     * Temporarily pause use of their profile.
     * Permanently delete their profile, with clear explanation of what will change (“Agents will stop personalizing based on your past settings.”).

## Profile Builder: What to Capture

A robust, user-centered profile builder should focus on **few, high-signal dimensions**, avoiding overwhelming questionnaires.

### 1. Identity & Role

* Title, team, domain.
* Optional multiple “hats”:

  * “I sometimes use this as a manager” vs. “as an individual contributor.”
* Use to:

  * Calibrate vocabulary.
  * Choose examples relevant to the user’s world (e.g., design vs. finance vs. engineering).

### 2. Goals & Outcomes

* “What should AI help you achieve?”

  * Task goals: “Draft better emails,” “Summarize meetings,” “Analyze product usage.”
  * Development goals: “Improve storytelling,” “Learn advanced analytics.”
* Prioritize:

  * “Optimize for: speed / quality / learning / experimentation.”
* These goals can inform:

  * Suggestions, feature prioritization, and how the AI frames trade-offs (e.g., speed vs. thoroughness).

### 3. Knowledge & Skill Levels

* Self-ratings for relevant domains (e.g., “AI/ML,” “SQL,” “product analytics,” “security,” “design systems”).
* Preferences:

  * “Explain concepts like I’m new.”
  * “Assume deep expertise but flag when you think I might be missing a concept.”
* Used to:

  * Adjust explanation depth and avoid condescending or over-technical responses.

### 4. Working Style & Format

* Detail level:

  * “Short summaries first; details on demand.”
  * “Deep, step-by-step explanations by default.”
* Preferred structures:

  * Bullets, tables, diagrams, code blocks, pseudo-code, story-like narratives.
* Interaction preferences:

  * “Start by asking clarifying questions.”
  * “Propose a plan before executing.”

### 5. Constraints & Guardrails

* Time, budget, and risk preferences (within global safety limits):

  * “I usually have 10–15 minutes,” “Avoid suggestions that require travel,” etc.
* Boundary preferences:

  * “Avoid suggesting third-party tools unless necessary.”
  * “Don’t mention competitor products in generated customer-facing content,” if relevant.

### 6. Tools, Stacks & Ecosystem

* Primary tools (e.g., Jira, GitHub, Figma, Notion).
* Tech stack or domain stack (e.g., React, Python, Salesforce, HubSpot).
* Integration hints:

  * “Prefer generating Jira tickets over long email threads.”
  * “When drafting specs, follow existing template X.”

### 7. Accessibility & Communication

* Language & locale.
* Reading preferences:

  * “Short paragraphs,” “Use headings,” “Highlight key actions.”
* Neurodiversity / cognitive support (if user opts to share):

  * “Avoid metaphor-heavy explanations,” “Provide clear, numbered steps,” etc.

## Presets & Templates

* **Role-based profile starters**

  * “Product Manager starting out,” “Senior Engineer,” “Customer Support Agent,” “Student/Intern.”
* **Goal-based templates**

  * “Improve my skills,” “Automate repetitive tasks,” “Make better decisions with data.”
* **Workspace-aware templates**

  * When joining a workspace, suggest profile fields based on others in similar roles, without exposing their private data.

All templates should be:

* Clearly framed as **starting points**, not rigid categories.
* Easily editable and overridable by the user.

## Integration with the AI Ecosystem

### Composition with Personas (8.2)

The runtime configuration for any interaction might look like:

1. **Org guardrails & policies** (non-negotiable safety/compliance).
2. **Agent Persona (8.2)** – “How does this agent normally behave?”
3. **User Preference & Context Profile (8.5)** – “Who is this user, what do they want, how do they like to work?”
4. **Conversation-specific instructions** – “For this task, do X.”

The UI should:

* Show that both **agent persona** and **user profile** are in play.
* Allow quick inspection: “Agent persona: ‘Support Specialist.’ Your profile: ‘Senior designer, prefers concise summaries.’”

### Scope & Inheritance

* **Per-user global profile** is the default.
* Agents may apply a **filtered view** of the profile:

  * E.g., the meeting assistant ignores tooling preferences but heavily uses goals and working style.
* Workspaces can add **workspace-specific goals** to the user’s profile in that context (“Within this product team, I’m focused on X”).

### Interaction with Memory (8.1)

* **Profile ≠ Memory of specific events**:

  * Profile: stable, user-curated facts (“I’m a designer,” “I like concise answers”).
  * Memory: derived/past interactions (“We discussed Feature A yesterday,” “You preferred option 2 last time.”)
* Provide distinct surfaces:

  * “My AI Profile” for stable preferences and identity.
  * “Memory & History” for past conversations and artifacts.
* Users can change their mind about profile facts **without editing history**, and vice versa.

## Maintenance, Governance & Feedback

### User-Level Feedback Loops

* After responses, lightweight check-ins:

  * “Did this match your working style?” [Yes/No] [Adjust Profile]
* Surface profile-driven behavior:

  * “Explaining this in more depth because your profile says you’re new to SQL. [Change].”
* Provide simple history:

  * Recent profile changes and one-click undo for mistakes.

### Admin & Org Controls

* By default, User Profiles are **personal**.

* Admin controls might include:

  * Setting **which fields exist** (e.g., “goals,” “tools”) and which are **opt-in**.
  * Defining which agents can access which profile fields.
  * Auditing *access to the profile* (who/what uses it) without revealing sensitive content unnecessarily.

* For regulated environments:

  * Allow orgs to **disable or restrict** certain profile fields (e.g., sensitive attributes).

### Evolution Over Time

* Gentle reminders:

  * “You haven’t updated your goals in 6 months—still accurate?”
* Versioning:

  * Optional version history in enterprise environments.
* Deprecation:

  * Ability to retire old role contexts or goals when the user changes jobs or responsibilities.

---

## Transparency & Control Indicators

* **In-chat “Using your profile” indicator**

  * Chip or text near the conversation header (e.g., “Personalized using your AI Profile”).
  * Clicking reveals: which fields were used (“Role,” “Goals,” “Detail preference”), and how they influence the response.

* **Disclosure on sensitive use**

  * When a sensitive field impacts behavior, surface that:

    * “Using your accessibility preferences to simplify this explanation.”

* **Immediate opt-out**

  * A per-conversation toggle:

    * “Pause personalization from my profile for this thread.”
  * Global setting:

    * “Disable AI Profile across all agents.”

* **Data usage explanations**

  * Clear language on:

    * What is stored.
    * Where it is stored.
    * How it is (not) used for training beyond this product.
    * How to export or delete it.

## Use Cases

### 1. Multi-Agent “Team of Assistants”

A user configures an AI Profile:

* Role: Principal Product Designer.
* Goals: “Ship better specs faster,” “Improve stakeholder alignment.”
* Working style: “Start with options; prefer visuals and tables,” “Call out risks.”

The design assistant, analytics assistant, and meeting assistant all use this profile. The analytics assistant explains metrics in design-relevant terms; the meeting assistant highlights action items involving design decisions; the design assistant formats outputs as specs and diagrams.

The user doesn’t need to repeat who they are or how they work to each new agent.

---

### 2. Learning & Upskilling Companion

A user sets:

* Goal: “Become proficient in SQL within 3 months.”
* Skill level: “Beginner.”
* Working style: “Use lots of small practice exercises and analogies.”

The AI respects this across docs, chat, and embedded helpers. When the user asks for help on product analytics, the assistant introduces relevant SQL examples, tracks progress, and uses gradually more advanced exercises. Periodically, it asks whether to update the skill level and profile.

---

### 3. Accessibility-Aware Experience

A user opts into:

* Plain language.
* Step-by-step breakdowns.
* Short paragraphs and strong headings.
* Reduced reliance on color in visual explanations.

Across all agents (support, documentation, analytics insights), content is structured accordingly. When a new agent is added to the product, the user is asked if they want to apply their AI Profile. They can confirm with a single click, ensuring consistent accessibility without reconfiguring.

---

### 4. Work vs. Personal Contexts

A user has:

* **Work profile:** Senior engineer, corporate tools, specific work goals.
* **Personal profile:** Parent planning family trips, personal learning goals, consumer tools.

In the main work product, the “Work profile” is active by default. On a personal assistant surface (e.g., a separate “Personal space” tab), the user switches to the personal profile. Agents clearly show which profile is active to avoid cross-contamination (“Using your *Personal* profile”).

---

## Variants

* **Single global profile vs. multiple profiles**

  * Simple products: one profile per user.
  * Complex ecosystems: multiple profiles per user, each bound to contexts (workspaces, tenants, use cases).

* **Implicit vs. explicit seeding**

  * Start with implicit inferences (e.g., from job title), but **always surface an explicit editable profile** rather than keeping it hidden.

* **Lightweight vs. rich profiles**

  * Consumer tools: short, goal-focused profile (goal, language, detail level).
  * Enterprise tools: richer, role-based profiles with multiple sections and stricter governance.

## Data, Privacy & Compliance Considerations

* **Data minimization**

  * Only ask for what you need to genuinely improve the experience.
  * Make sensitive fields optional and clearly marked.

* **Consent and control**

  * Always let users:

    * See which data is stored.
    * Edit or delete it.
    * Decide whether each agent can access it.

* **Access boundaries**

  * Enforce that profiles are **per-user** and are not shared across accounts.
  * In multi-tenant systems, respect tenant boundaries—even if a user has accounts in multiple organizations.

* **Separation from other data**

  * Store profile configuration separately from conversational logs where possible.
  * Ensure that profile data is not inadvertently exposed via logs, exports, or APIs.

* **Regulatory alignment**

  * Be careful with protected attributes (e.g., health, religion, demographics).
  * If users choose to share such information for accessibility or personalization, treat it as highly sensitive and provide explicit handling and deletion.

## Risks & Anti-Patterns

* **Over-asking up front**

  * Long, mandatory questionnaires before the user has experienced value will cause drop-off and distrust.

* **Shadow profiles**

  * Inferring deep profiles behind the scenes without surfacing them to users, or applying personalization from hidden sources.

* **Irrevocable inferences**

  * Treating early or mistaken self-assessments as truth forever, without prompts to revise (“You said you were a beginner; never revisiting that.”).

* **Profile misuse**

  * Using profile information beyond expected uses (e.g., for targeting, scoring, or decisions unrelated to user-visible personalization) without explicit consent.

* **Confusing profiles with access control**

  * Using profile fields (like job title) as the only basis for permissions, instead of proper roles and policies.

* **Agents silently ignoring the profile**

  * If the system claims to use the profile but responses clearly don’t reflect it, trust quickly erodes.

## Implementation Notes

### UX & Product

* Start with **small, high-impact fields**:

  * Role, goal, detail level, and expertise are often enough to unlock value.
* Introduce the profile **when it helps**, not at signup:

  * After the user has felt some friction (“You keep asking for shorter answers…”).
* Use **examples and previews**:

  * Show “Before vs. After” examples when changing preferences (e.g., more detailed vs. more succinct).
* Separate “Profile” from “Memory” in the UI:

  * Different entry points, different mental models.

### Engineering & System Design

* Represent the profile as a **structured object**, not just raw text.

  * E.g., `role`, `goals`, `expertise`, `format_preferences`, etc.
* Build a **composition layer**:

  * That merges: org guardrails + agent persona + user profile + task instructions in a deterministic order.
* Implement **field-level access control**:

  * Some agents may only read certain fields; sensitive fields may be restricted.
* Ensure **idempotence**:

  * Applying the same profile multiple times should not result in conflicting or duplicated instructions.
* Log **identifiers, not raw profile content**, when possible:

  * For analytics, associate responses with `profile_version_id` rather than storing full profile text in every log.

## Metrics & Evaluation

* **Adoption**

  * % of active users with a completed profile.
  * Fields filled vs. skipped.

* **Friction reduction**

  * Reduction in prompts containing repetitive self-description.
  * Decrease in “explain my role/skill” statements over time for users with profiles.

* **Trust & satisfaction**

  * Survey items like “The AI understands my goals and working style.”
  * Higher task satisfaction for users with active profiles vs. those without.

* **Quality & alignment**

  * Fewer complaints about tone mismatch or miscalibrated explanations.
  * Increased success rates for multi-step tasks where context matters.

* **Governance & safety**

  * Incidents involving misused or exposed profile data.
  * Time-to-remediation for profile deletion and corrections.

---

## Checklist for Teams

* [ ] Is there a clear, dedicated surface for users to view and edit their AI Profile?
* [ ] Do agents clearly indicate when they are using the user’s profile, and which parts?
* [ ] Can users easily pause or disable profile-based personalization per conversation or globally?
* [ ] Are profile fields minimal, high-signal, and clearly optional where sensitive?
* [ ] Is the composition of org guardrails, agent persona, user profile, and task instructions deterministic and understandable?
* [ ] Are profile data and conversation history separated and governed differently?
* [ ] Can users update, export, and delete their profile, with predictable downstream effects?
* [ ] Are analytics and logging designed to respect privacy (e.g., using profile IDs rather than duplicating full content)?
* [ ] Do you have safeguards against hidden or shadow profiling?
