# 6.3 Semantic Highlighting of Uncertainty

Visually and interactively expose uncertain portions of AI-generated content so human reviewers can quickly identify, inspect, and act on areas that require judgment.

*Last updated December 2025*

---

## Overview

Semantic Highlighting of Uncertainty is a pattern that overlays visual cues on specific words, phrases, sentences, or steps in AI-generated output where the system has low or ambiguous confidence. Instead of presenting a monolithic answer that appears uniformly reliable, the UI differentiates between “likely reliable” and “needs scrutiny” content.

This pattern frequently appears in:

* AI copilots inside productivity tools (docs, spreadsheets, email, IDEs).
* Agentic workflows that draft contracts, summarize accounts, or propose actions.
* Analytics or BI assistants that infer trends and recommendations.

The core idea is to use fine-grained, contextual signals—such as dotted underlines, soft highlights, or inline badges—to direct expert attention to the most questionable parts of an AI result. Interacting with a highlight reveals why the system is uncertain and offers targeted actions such as refining inputs, requesting alternatives, or escalating to a human expert. This increases trust not by claiming that the AI is always right, but by being explicit about where it may be wrong.

### Embedded Example (for demos / pattern gallery)

Imagine a contract review copilot embedded in a B2B SaaS legal platform:

1. The AI generates a redlined version of a master services agreement.
2. Most clauses look normal, but three phrases are subtly highlighted with a dotted underline and pale amber background.
3. Hovering over a highlight displays a tooltip:

   * “Low confidence: training data contains conflicting interpretations for similar indemnification language.”
   * Buttons: **Clarify clause**, **Show source references**, **Assign to senior counsel**.
4. Clicking **Clarify clause** opens a side panel where the AI proposes two alternative phrasings and explains trade-offs.
5. Once counsel manually edits and approves a clause, the highlight collapses into a small “Reviewed” badge for auditability.

This example can be visualized as an interactive overlay on top of a standard document viewer, demonstrating how highlights focus attention without taking over the UI.

## Problem

Without semantic uncertainty cues, AI output is often perceived as uniformly confident. This creates several issues in both B2B and B2C applications:

* **Automation bias and over-trust**
  Users may over-rely on AI responses because the interface does not distinguish between “plausible guess” and “strongly supported conclusion.”

* **Cognitive overload in long outputs**
  When the AI produces long-form documents, analyses, or multi-step plans, reviewing every part at equal depth is impractical. Important ambiguities are easily missed.

* **Invisible model and data limitations**
  Global disclaimers (“This may be wrong”) are too coarse. They do not reveal *where* limitations are most impactful or *why* the system is uncertain.

* **Inefficient human review workflows**
  Reviewers lack clear triage signals to decide which sections to inspect first, slowing down high-stakes processes such as approvals, compliance checks, or customer responses.

These issues reduce trust and can either cause under-use (users ignore AI entirely) or over-use (blind acceptance), both of which counteract the intended value of agentic systems.

---

## When to Use This Pattern

* **High- or medium-stakes decision support**
  Legal review, pricing recommendations, financial summaries, medical documentation support, security assessments, compliance checks, HR or policy decisions.

* **Complex, dense, or lengthy outputs**
  Contracts, technical specs, strategy docs, long chat transcripts, financial reports, or multi-step action plans where complete line-by-line review is unrealistic.

* **AI agents that can trigger or propose real-world actions**
  Systems that draft emails, update CRM records, modify infrastructure, or schedule workflows, where incorrect steps can have operational or reputational impact.

* **Scenarios with known uncertainty sources**
  Cases with sparse data, conflicting sources, domain or language mismatches, or limited grounding where the system can estimate and localize uncertainty.

---

## Probably Overkill When...

* **Low-stakes ideation and exploration**
  Naming explorations, casual brainstorming, storytelling, or moodboarding, where minor inaccuracies or ambiguities are acceptable.

* **Short, atomic responses**
  Single-sentence answers or simple lookups where confidence is already obvious or the risk is minimal, and a global confidence badge or disclaimer is sufficient.

* **Highly deterministic or validated workflows**
  Flows where the system already uses strict validation, exhaustive search, or rules-based checks that guarantee correctness for the domain.

* **Unreliable or uncalibrated uncertainty estimates**
  Situations where the underlying confidence signals are too noisy or misleading, leading to fake precision and erosion of trust.

## Pattern Anatomy

Semantic Highlighting of Uncertainty is layered on top of existing surfaces such as chat messages, document editors, tables, dashboards, or code editors. It should feel additive and contextual, not like an entirely separate experience.

### Entry Points

* **Primary entry point**
  AI-generated message or document view (e.g., “AI draft” in a side panel, or an agent’s suggested answer in a support console) with highlights visible by default or enabled via a prominent **“Show uncertainty”** toggle.

* **Secondary entry point**
  Toolbar or header controls on the AI response:

  * Toggle to **Show/Hide highlights**.
  * Drop-down to adjust sensitivity (e.g., “Only high uncertainty”, “High + medium”).

* **Contextual entry point**
  Inline chips, banners, or summary indicators such as:

  * “3 sections marked as low confidence.”
  * “Some parts of this analysis rely on sparse data.”
    Clicking these indicators scrolls or jumps to the highlighted segments.

### Core Item / Object

The primary unit in this pattern is the **Uncertainty Marker**, associated with a text span or step.

* **Label**
  Communicates severity and type, for example:

  * “Low confidence”
  * “Ambiguous translation”
  * “Sparse data”
  * “Conflicting sources”

* **Description / statement**
  Short, explanatory text that clarifies why the system is unsure, ideally grounded in evidence or model behavior, such as:

  * “Model has not seen many similar clauses in training data.”
  * “Source documents disagree on this figure.”
  * “Trend calculation is based on < 10 data points.”

* **Controls**
  Inline or nearby actions that turn uncertainty into next steps, such as:

  * **Clarify this section** (ask the AI to refine or rephrase).
  * **Show supporting evidence** (open citations, source snippets, or data samples).
  * **See alternatives** (present multiple candidate phrasings or analyses).
  * **Escalate / Assign for human review** (add to a queue, mention a teammate, create a task).

* **Optional metadata**
  Additional structured data to aid prioritization and later analysis:

  * Uncertainty level (e.g., low/medium/high) or a bucketed score.
  * Reason category (e.g., “data quality”, “out-of-domain”, “hallucination risk”).
  * Affected entities or fields (e.g., “pricing”, “SLAs”, “dates”).
  * Resolution status (“Unreviewed”, “Reviewed”, “Overridden by [role]”).

### Behavior & Lifecycle

1. **Initial generation and scoring**

   * The AI produces an output (text, steps, suggestions).
   * A separate component computes token-, phrase-, or step-level uncertainty using model confidence scores, retrieval coverage, self-consistency checks, or ensemble estimates.
   * The system segments the output and associates each segment with an uncertainty level and reason category.

2. **Rendering and initial display**

   * The output is rendered with subtle visual cues on segments above configured thresholds (e.g., medium and high uncertainty).
   * An optional summary indicator communicates the overall situation, for example: “This answer contains 1 high and 2 medium uncertainty segments.”

3. **User interaction with highlights**

   * Hovering or focusing a highlight shows a tooltip or popover with:

     * Severity label.
     * Short explanatory description.
     * Key actions (Clarify, Show evidence, See alternatives).
   * Keyboard focus moves through highlights in reading order, and shortcuts can jump between them.

4. **Deep inspection and action**

   * Clicking a highlight can open a side panel that:

     * Shows relevant source passages or queries used.
     * Renders multiple candidate alternatives.
     * Provides controls to rewrite, comment, or route to another role.
   * Taking an action (e.g., accepting an alternative, editing text) updates the main content.

5. **Resolution and state update**

   * Once a segment is reviewed, the marker can:

     * Downgrade severity (e.g., from “High uncertainty” to “Reviewed”).
     * Collapse into a small badge or annotation to avoid clutter.
     * Retain metadata for audit (who resolved it, when, and how).

6. **Persistence and auditing**

   * Uncertainty markers and their resolution history are stored with the object (contract version, analysis run, ticket, etc.).
   * Audit trails can show which ambiguous sections were explicitly reviewed versus left in their original form.

7. **Feedback loop and calibration**

   * Interaction data (e.g., which highlights users override, ignore, or escalate) feeds back into updating thresholds and scoring functions.
   * Over time, the system can reduce false positives and focus on the most meaningful uncertainties for each domain or role.

## States & Variants

### States

* **No highlights**
  No segments exceed the uncertainty threshold, or the feature is disabled. The UI may still show a global confidence state.

* **Active highlights (unreviewed)**
  Default state when segments are flagged. Visual intensity should correlate with severity but avoid alarm fatigue.

* **Reviewed / resolved**
  Highlights that have been explicitly reviewed shrink into compact badges or annotations, indicating completion without losing traceability.

* **Unavailable / degraded**
  When uncertainty computation fails or is unavailable (e.g., offline mode), a simple message explains that highlights are not shown and why.

### Common Variants

* **Inline-only variant**
  Simple dotted underlines or shaded spans with hover tooltips—low implementation complexity, good for early-stage integration.

* **Inline + side panel variant**
  Rich explanation and actions in a side panel linked to the highlight; better for complex tasks such as legal or financial review.

* **Summary-first variant**
  A top-level “Uncertainty summary” card with a list of affected sections and jump links, particularly useful for long documents.

* **Step-level variant**
  In agentic flows with multi-step plans or tools, each step carries a confidence label and can be expanded to show uncertainty sources.

## Content & Microcopy Guidelines

* **Use neutral, descriptive language**
  Prefer “Low confidence,” “Needs review,” “Ambiguous,” or “Based on limited data” over accusatory or alarming wording.

* **Explain reasons, not just levels**
  Short, precise reasons build understanding:

  * “Few recent data points after 2023.”
  * “Sources disagree on this figure.”
  * “Model has low familiarity with this jurisdiction.”

* **Avoid fake precision**
  Numeric percentages should be used only if well-calibrated and explainable. In many products, three or four tiers (“Low / Medium / High / Not evaluated”) are easier to understand.

* **Connect to concrete actions**
  Every uncertainty description should naturally lead into one or more actions such as clarify, compare alternatives, or escalate.

* **Localize carefully**
  Severity descriptors must maintain meaning in translations; avoid idioms that may imply blame or certainty that is not intended.

---

## Accessibility Considerations

* **Avoid color-only signaling**
  Pair color with patterns, icons, and explicit text labels so that uncertainty remains visible to users with color vision deficiencies.

* **Respect reading order and focus**
  Highlights must be reachable via keyboard and announced in a logical sequence. Screen readers should announce both the underlying content and its uncertainty status.

* **Clear, concise announcements**
  For assistive technologies, use short, structured phrases like:

  * “Low confidence segment: [text sample]. Reason: conflicting data.”

* **Configurable intensity**
  Consider settings for reducing visual intensity for users sensitive to visual clutter or motion, especially if highlights appear dynamically.

## Instrumentation & Metrics

To validate and refine this pattern, instrument both behavior and outcomes:

* **Interaction metrics**

  * Percentage of AI outputs that contain highlights.
  * Hover and click rates on highlighted segments.
  * Usage of actions such as “Clarify,” “Show evidence,” and “Escalate.”

* **Review effectiveness**

  * Error detection rate in highlighted vs. non-highlighted segments.
  * Time to approval or resolution in workflows with and without highlighting.
  * Reduction in downstream corrections, tickets, or rework.

* **Trust calibration**

  * Survey-based measures of perceived reliability and controllability.
  * Behavioral indicators such as over-acceptance vs. healthy skepticism.

* **Model calibration feedback**

  * False positive rate (segments flagged but rarely corrected).
  * False negative rate (serious issues in non-highlighted segments).
    These should feed into adjusting thresholds and improving uncertainty estimation methods.

## Implementation Notes

### Uncertainty Signals (Backend)

* **Token-level or span-level confidence**
  Derived from model probabilities, log probabilities, or other internal signals aggregated over a span.

* **Self-consistency and sampling**
  Compare multiple sampled outputs; segments with high disagreement can be marked as uncertain.

* **Retrieval and evidence coverage**
  In retrieval-augmented systems, highlight statements not strongly grounded in retrieved documents or where supporting evidence is weak or missing.

* **Domain rules and heuristics**
  Overlay domain-specific checks (e.g., unusual contract terms, out-of-policy values, statistical anomalies) to adjust or override model-driven uncertainty.

* **Calibration and thresholds**
  Use offline evaluation and human labeling to calibrate thresholds for “Low / Medium / High” and ensure they align with actual correctness rates and domain risk.

### UI & Interaction

* **Visual hierarchy**
  Use subtle default styling (e.g., light background, dotted underline, neutral icon) and reserve stronger colors only for truly high-risk segments.

* **User control**
  Provide controls to:

  * Toggle highlights on/off.
  * Filter by severity (e.g., show only high uncertainty).
  * Expand or collapse all details.

* **Performance considerations**
  Generate and apply highlights in a way that does not noticeably slow down rendering, especially in long documents or streaming chat responses.

* **Consistency across surfaces**
  Apply the same styling and terminology across chat, docs, dashboards, and other surfaces to build recognition and reduce cognitive load.

## Example Use Cases

### Contract Review Agent

In a legal workflow application:

* The AI suggests changes to a vendor agreement.
* Clauses involving indemnification, liability caps, or jurisdiction are analyzed for confidence based on training data similarity and rule-based checks.
* Ambiguous or unusual phrases are highlighted with a dotted underline and amber background.
* Hovering reveals: “Low confidence: uncommon phrasing for limitation of liability in this industry.”
* Actions allow legal counsel to:

  * Open similar clauses from past approved contracts.
  * Generate alternative phrasings.
  * Assign the clause to a specialist for review.

Result: lawyers focus on high-risk areas first, while standard boilerplate remains visually quiet.

### Translation & Localization AI

In a multilingual collaboration tool:

* The AI translates a long product announcement from English into Japanese, Spanish, and German.
* Idiomatic and culturally nuanced phrases (e.g., metaphors, humor, wordplay) receive lower model confidence due to less consistent training examples.
* These phrases are highlighted in the translated text with an inline “Ambiguous translation” chip.
* On click, the UI displays:

  * Alternative translations for different tones (formal, casual, marketing-focused).
  * Explanations such as “Source phrase uses a culture-specific idiom.”
  * A “Request native review” option that posts the segment into a localization review queue.

Result: localization teams can efficiently focus attention on the segments most likely to need human refinement.

### Data Analysis & BI Assistant

In a business intelligence platform with an AI analysis agent:

* The AI generates a narrative summary of revenue trends and forecasts.
* Sections based on sparse data, heavy seasonality, or outliers are labeled with higher uncertainty scores.
* Sentences like “Revenue will likely grow 20–25% next quarter” are highlighted in the narrative.
* A tooltip explains: “Forecast based on limited historical data (8 weeks) and high volatility.”
* Actions let analysts:

  * Drill into the underlying chart and data subset.
  * Adjust filters, date ranges, or model assumptions.
  * Mark the forecast as “Not for external use” or attach an explanatory note.

Result: analysts understand where the AI is extrapolating aggressively and can adjust or qualify recommendations before sharing.

### Code Copilot in Developer Tools

In an IDE with AI code completion:

* The AI suggests a multi-line function implementation.
* Lines that interact with security-sensitive APIs or complex concurrency constructs are assigned higher uncertainty.
* These lines receive subtle highlight cues (e.g., dotted underline plus an “Review carefully” icon).
* Hover text explains: “Low confidence: limited examples in training data for this library version.”
* Actions let developers request alternative implementations, show documentation links, or generate tests around the highlighted code.

Result: developers’ attention is directed to the riskiest portions of suggestions, improving review quality and reducing subtle defects.

## Related Patterns

* **Global Confidence Badges**
  Single overall confidence indicator for an answer or plan, complementary to fine-grained highlighting.

* **Evidence Highlighting / Grounded Citations**
  Pattern that visually ties generated statements to supporting source snippets or data tables.

* **Human Review Gates & Queues**
  Workflow pattern for routing uncertain or high-risk outputs to specific roles for approval.

* **Alternatives & Branching Suggestions**
  Mechanism that presents multiple candidate phrasings or plans, often triggered from uncertainty markers.

* **Clarification & Follow-up Prompts**
  Pattern where the system asks clarifying questions when uncertainty is driven by ambiguous or incomplete user input.

Together, these patterns form a toolkit for making agentic AI systems more transparent, reviewable, and aligned with human judgment in both B2B and B2C applications.
